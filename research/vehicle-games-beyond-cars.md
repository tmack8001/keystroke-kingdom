## 1. What the car arc already covers, so this file doesn't repeat it

`racing-games-by-era.md` §15's recommended spine already contains **two motorcycles and one
anti-gravity racer** — the "not just cars" direction was latent before this lane started:

- **[[excitebike]]** (Nintendo, 1984) — **motocross**, side-on, with landing angle and an overheat
  gauge. The bike class is already represented at the 8-bit station.
- **[[hang-on]]** (Sega, 1985) — a **motorcycle**, on a ride-on cabinet you physically lean. The
  bike class is already represented at the Super Scaler station too.
- **[[f-zero]]** (Nintendo, 1990) — an **anti-gravity racer**. The "futuristic hovering vehicle"
  class is taken.

So the genuinely *new* classes available to this file are: **human-powered (bicycle)**,
**watercraft**, **rail**, and — judged and rejected below — **anti-grav again** and **snow/skate**.

## 2. The verdicts, class by class

### ✅ Bicycle — **[[paperboy]]** (Atari Games, April 1985). RECOMMENDED.

The one vehicle in the whole survey **a child could actually own.** Its arcade cabinet has a real
**bicycle handlebar** (a modified Star Wars yoke) with a throw button on each side, **pushed
forward to accelerate and pulled back to brake** — the most tactile control panel in this research,
and the only one whose real-world equivalent is in the family garage.

It is also a **visual** step the car arc lacks: an **oblique/isometric suburban street**, not
another rear-view road. And tonally it is a lovely breath after a decade of race cars — the goal is
*deliver the papers*, on an ordinary Sunday morning.

**Caution:** the comedy is largely **property damage** (windows, garden gnomes), which wants a light
touch under the house rules. Nothing in it is violent or frightening and nobody is ever hurt.

**Precision note:** the **title screen says 1984**; the cabinet **released April 1985**. Both dates
are "right" — say which you mean. Hardware: Atari System 2, a **10 MHz DEC T-11** CPU (a
PDP-11-architecture chip in an arcade cabinet — a nice minicomputer-heritage detail for a game that
already loves those) plus a 2.2 MHz MOS 6502 for sound.

**Punishment mechanics:** a finite paper supply; missing subscribers loses customers; crashing into
obstacles costs a life; Wednesday-to-Sunday weeks with a training course between them.

**References — Paperboy:** see [[paperboy]]. **Sourcing honesty: no URL on that page was fetched
this session** — the facts are consistent across three independent results, but re-verify before
child-facing text.

### ✅ Watercraft — **[[wave-race-64]]** (Nintendo EAD, September/November 1996). RECOMMENDED.

**A genuine generational step, not a reskin.** The car arc's rendering ladder covers per-surface
*friction* ([[sega-rally-championship]], 1995 — asphalt vs gravel vs mud change your **grip**).
Wave Race 64 does something categorically different: **the ground itself moves and has a shape that
changes moment to moment.** The N64's alpha blending makes water *"simultaneously transparent and
reflective"*, and the swell physically drives the rider — a wave lifting the nose mid-turn pushes
you offline. A theme author gets something to draw that no car cabinet offers.

Its origin is a nice one: a Nintendo EAD programmer's **wave-programming tech demo** on the new
hardware caught **Shigeru Miyamoto's** eye and became the game.

**And its core loop is a One-Rule gift.** Pass red buoys on the right and yellow on the left, and
each correct buoy lights a **power arrow** — up to five — increasing acceleration. The reward is
**additive**, and the ordinary cost of a mistake is simply **losing the bonus you had built**.
That is the same shape as [[mario-kart]]'s coins, which `racing-games-by-era.md` §12 already flags
as the most reusable idea in the genre — independently arrived at, four years later, on water.

**Punishment mechanics to strip:** missing **five** buoys in a race, or straying outside the pink
boundary buoys for **more than five seconds**, is **disqualification**. **ESRB E, no descriptors.**

**References — Wave Race 64:** see [[wave-race-64]] (Wikipedia **[fetched]**; ESRB record
**[fetched]**; MobyGames).

### 🟡 Rail — **[[densha-de-go]]** (Taito, arcade **March 1997**). FOOTNOTE, not a station.

Fascinating, and *almost* perfect for a different reason than you'd expect. Its virtue is that it
is the one vehicle game in this entire survey where **the goal is accuracy rather than velocity**:
obey the signals, obey the speed limits, and stop **exactly** at the mark on the platform, on time.
If Act 7's thesis is *"speed AND accuracy"*, Densha de Go! is **the accuracy half made literal.**

Its **punishment mechanic is also the most interesting in either research file**: a **bonus-second**
economy where accurate stops and on-time arrivals **add** seconds and mistakes deduct them — *"when
all of the bonus seconds are exhausted, the game ends."* **The timer is a currency you earn by
being careful, not a countdown you merely survive.** That is structurally almost identical to the
reframe both files keep recommending ("typing adds road").

**Why it is still a footnote:** the cabinet's whole identity is a **physical master controller**
(brake handle and throttle levers modelled on a 205-series EMU cab) rather than anything
keyboard-shaped; the cultural context — Japanese commuter-rail timetabling — needs more explaining
to a Western six-year-old than it repays; and it adds **no rendering step** the act lacks.

**Correction:** it is routinely dated **1996**. That is the year it was **demonstrated**, at the
1996 Amusement Machine Show. It **shipped in March 1997**. The PlayStation port (December 1997)
sold over a million copies.

**Railroad Tycoon** (Sid Meier, 1990) was also considered and is **out**: it is a **management**
game, not a vehicle game — you never drive anything — so it belongs to a SimCity-adjacent
conversation, and [[simcity-2000]] is already **Level 16**.

**References — Densha de Go!:** see [[densha-de-go]] (Wikipedia **[fetched]**).

### ❌ Anti-gravity again — **Wipeout** (Psygnosis, 1995). OUT.

A landmark, and **not a generational step this act needs.** [[f-zero]] already owns the
anti-gravity racer class in the car arc, and the *rendering* step Wipeout represents —
texture-mapped PS1 polygons — is already covered by [[ridge-racer]] (1993) and [[daytona-usa]]
(1994). What Wipeout genuinely contributed was **cultural**: Designers Republic's graphic identity
and the rave/techno soundtrack that made the PlayStation seem adult. That is a **parent-facing**
note, not a cabinet.

Its ESRB rating is the pre-1998 **"Kids to Adults"** band — which is fine, but is also a reminder
that a 1995 rating does not map cleanly onto today's E.

**References:** [Wipeout (video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wipeout_(video_game)) — 1995 Psygnosis PlayStation release; F3600 anti-gravity league; Designers Republic involvement. *(search result only, not fetched)* · [30 years of Wipeout — BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/wipeout-psygnosis-sony-playstation) — institutional (British Film Institute) cultural history of the game and its rave-music context. *(search result only, not fetched)* · [WipEout (USA) — Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/psx_wipeout) — archival record of the PlayStation release. *(search result only, not fetched)*

### ❌ Tanks / military vehicles — **[[battlezone]]** (Atari, 1980). OUT, though it hurts.

The hardest "no" in this research. Battlezone is **widely called the first true first-person 3D
arcade game**, it is **vector graphics** — a rendering technique Keystroke Kingdom owns *nowhere* —
and it is era-perfect. But the vehicle is a **tank**, the verb is *destroy enemy tanks*, and the
most historically interesting thing about it is the **Bradley Trainer**, a US Army gunnery
simulator. Three strikes on register for a children's game.

**The vector era is not lost, though** — [[lunar-lander-1979]] supplies it: same year, same Atari,
same technique, and the subject is **landing on the Moon** instead of shooting. It is covered in
[flight-simulators-by-era.md](flight-simulators-by-era.md) §8.

**Keep for the parents:** lead designer **Ed Rotberg** initially refused to work on the military
variant and agreed only on the condition that he would never be assigned a military project again.
Only **two** Bradley Trainers were built. That is a first-rate conscience-in-engineering note,
adjacent to the hacker-ethic material in [hacker-culture.md](hacker-culture.md) — a *history card
for grown-ups*, not a cabinet.

**References — Battlezone:** see [[battlezone]] (Wikipedia **[fetched]**; KLOV returned HTTP 403).

### ❌ Snow / skate / BMX. OUT, and briefly.

1080° Snowboarding, SSX, the Tony Hawk line: these are **trick** games, scored on execution of
named manoeuvres. That is a genuinely different genre from racing, they add no rendering step the
act lacks, and several are not vehicle games at all (a skateboard is not a vehicle in the sense this
act means). **Not researched in depth this pass and nothing is asserted about them** — recorded as
a considered exclusion so a spec lane does not have to re-litigate it. `[unverified]`.

## 5. Punishment ledger — the classes in this file

Matching `racing-games-by-era.md` §12's format.

| Title | What the real game does to you | Why it violates the One Rule | Suggested reframe |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[paperboy]] (1985) | Finite papers; missing subscribers **loses customers**; crashing costs a life | Lives + a shrinking customer list = cumulative, visible decline | Customers are **gained, never lost**. A missed throw is just a throw you get to make again |
| [[wave-race-64]] (1996) | Missing one buoy **resets your power**; **five** missed buoys or 5s out of bounds = **disqualification** | Elimination mid-race | **Keep the power arrows exactly as they are** (additive bonus). Delete disqualification entirely — the course simply continues |
| [[densha-de-go]] (1997) | A **bonus-second** balance; mistakes deduct; **"when all of the bonus seconds are exhausted, the game ends"** | It is still, ultimately, a clock that can reach zero | **Almost right already** — make seconds **only ever accumulate**. Careful typing buys track; carelessness simply buys none |
| Wipeout (1995) | Weapons, and being knocked out of a race | Combat-adjacent | Not used — §2 |
| [[battlezone]] (1980) | Destroyed by enemy tanks | Combat; military-trainer heritage | Not used — §2 |

**The mechanic worth stealing from this file:** **Densha de Go!'s bonus-second economy.** Every
other timer in either research file is a countdown you survive. This one is a **balance you earn by
being precise** — which is, almost exactly, the mechanic both files keep independently proposing as
the One-Rule-safe conversion of an arcade clock. It existed in a real cabinet in 1997.

## 6. Corrections from this research pass (2026-09-12)

*(Flight and space corrections — fifteen of them — live in
[flight-simulators-by-era.md](flight-simulators-by-era.md) §14.)*

1. **"Densha de Go! is a 1996 game."** It was **demonstrated** at the 1996 Amusement Machine Show;
   the arcade cabinet **shipped March 1997**. Created-vs-shipped.
2. **"Paperboy is a 1984 game."** The **title screen says 1984**; the cabinet **released April
   1985**. Both figures are real — say which one you mean.
3. **"Wave Race 64 released 1 November 1996 in North America."** Wikipedia's article gives
   **4 November 1996**; the 1 November date circulates widely. Neither was independently arbitrated;
   prefer the fetched source.
4. **"Railroad Tycoon is the train game for a vehicle act."** It is a **management** game — you never
   drive. If a rail cabinet is wanted, it is [[densha-de-go]]; if a *management* cabinet is wanted,
   [[simcity-2000]] is already Level 16.
5. **"Wipeout is the obvious anti-gravity pick."** [[f-zero]] already holds that class in the car
   arc, and Wipeout's rendering step is covered by [[ridge-racer]] and [[daytona-usa]]. Its real
   contribution was cultural, which is a note rather than a cabinet.
