## The canon comparison (Trevor's childhood typing games)

**[[mario-teaches-typing|Mario Teaches Typing]] (Interplay, 1992)** — the only true comparable. Uses the same
proven progression we do: home row first, then reaches outward in finger-pairs (E/I → R/U
→ T/Y → W/O → Q/P, then bottom row). Same lineage as [[mavis-beacon|Mavis Beacon]], [[dance-mat-typing|Dance Mat Typing]],
[[typingclub|TypingClub]] — the ordering is physiological, not stylistic (see [[touch-typing-pedagogy]]). **Where we differ:** MTT wears
one skin the whole way; our one-distinct-cabinet-per-level + era-progressive graphics is
the upgrade. And MTT (like nearly every tutor) lumps `` ` - = [ ] ' \ `` into a single
"symbols" lesson or skips them; our dedicated Corner Cabinet levels (21-23) are MORE
thorough on the outer keys than the canon.

**[[bookworm-game|Bookworm]] (PopCap, 2003)** — not a typing tutor: word-BUILDING from letter tiles
(vocabulary/spelling), assumes you already type. We share its soul in one place — "typing
a real word you can read is a double win" — but nothing structural.

**Sports/drill typing games (the basketball one, late-90s edu-CD era)** — the citation
pass found the actual game: **[[slam-dunk-typing|Slam Dunk Typing]] (Creative Wonders, 1997)**, reissued as
**All-Star Typing: Basketball Edition (The Learning Company, 1999)**. Word-drill reskins:
type the word, sink the shot; miss and you brick it. No key-introduction sequence at all.
The instructive contrast: our One Rule means a wrong key never bricks the shot — the
letter waits. (Survival mode, added 2026-07-11, is the opt-in exception and even it never
punishes wrong keys — time pressure only.)

### References — the shared key-progression pedagogy

The home-row-first, finger-pair-outward ordering that MTT, Mavis Beacon, Dance Mat, and
TypingClub all share is the standard [[touch-typing-pedagogy|touch-typing curriculum]], documented in commercial
keyboarding textbooks and modern curricula alike (note: it's treated as uncontroversial
baseline practice in the literature — curricula document it; academic papers rarely argue
for it):

- [Gregg College Keyboarding & Document Processing (Ober, Johnson, Zimmerly) — McGraw-Hill](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/gregg-college-keyboarding-document-processing-gdp-lessons-1-120-main-text-ober-johnson/M9780073372198.html) —
  the 50+-year standard keyboarding textbook; Lesson 1 is touch-typing ASDF JKL;, home row first.
- [Gregg College Keyboarding & Document Processing — Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/greggcollegekeyb0000ober_b7j8) —
  borrowable full text of the same textbook for direct citation of lesson structure.
- [Typing Jungle Teacher Handbook — TypingClub (PDF)](https://static.typingclub.com/m/edclubdocs/media/pdf/typing-jungle-handbook.pdf) —
  TypingClub's teacher-facing curriculum: home-row-first, finger-to-key assignment,
  progressive outward sequencing.
- [Topic B: The Base Position — Key Concepts of Computer Studies (BCcampus open textbook)](https://opentextbc.ca/computerstudies/chapter/the-base-position/) —
  open textbook describing the home row as the anchor position from which all other keys are taught.

Historical footnote: touch typing was popularized (invention is disputed) by Frank Edward
McGurrin, who won the famous July 25, 1888 Cincinnati typing contest against Louis Traub
typing without looking at the keys; Bates Torrey coined "writing by touch" in 1889.
McGurrin should be cited as popularizer, not settled inventor.

- [Touch typing — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touch_typing) — 1888 contest,
  invented-vs-popularized dispute, Torrey's 1889 terminology.
- [Frank Edward McGurrin — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Edward_McGurrin) —
  biography; explicitly notes that whether he was first to touch-type "is disputed."

## Decisions locked during Act 5 planning (with reasoning)

*These are internal design decisions. Where a decision leans on an external factual
claim (letter frequency, decodable-word research, error-feedback research), the
reference blocks below each cluster carry the sources; the rest is project judgment.*

1. **Symbols before Shift.** (See [[touch-typing-pedagogy]] for how this and the next decision extend the canon.) `` ` - = [ ] \ ' `` are unshifted base keys; learn the
   location first, then Shift becomes the payoff that unlocks everything printed on the
   keys' upper halves. (Canon tutors under-teach this; we made it three levels.)
2. **The left/right pairing rhythm breaks at 21-23, on purpose.** Every letter level pairs
   one left + one right hand key. The leftover symbols cluster right-pinky (only `` ` `` is
   left-handed), so the Corner Cabinet levels are honestly framed as right-pinky-reach
   levels rather than forcing fake symmetry with review keys. Decision: accept anatomy.
3. **Backspace is taught as a skill, not a confession.** "Type it, then erase it" drills;
   Backspace counts TOWARD progress. Framing from product.md: "Backspace isn't failure.
   Every writer uses it."
4. **Case-sensitivity activates only when the expected character is uppercase** — a
   CapsLock'd child on levels 1-23 is never wrong for the machine's reasons.
5. **Modifier keydowns never count as mistakes.** Pressing Shift while expecting 'A' is
   the correct FIRST HALF of the action; the engine ignores all pure modifiers.
6. **The in-game keyboard guide is a true ANSI silhouette** (staggered rows, real key
   widths, fog-of-war for unlearned keys) — E/I must appear physically above D/K, because
   the on-screen map is teaching the finger map. Centered/filtered rows were tried and
   rejected (2026-07-10).
7. **Words are early-reader decodable** (3-5 letters, phonetic), per product.md's Word
   Bank Principle; equations on number levels (`2-1=1`) extend "typing is computation."
   Open question in docs/ROADMAP.md §5: educator review of the word banks.

### References — [[error-feedback-pedagogy|error treatment / feedback]] (supports decisions 3-5 and the One Rule)

The design stance — immediate, informational, non-punitive correction; the letter waits —
is consistent with motor-learning research on feedback (Knowledge of Results / Knowledge
of Performance) and with keyboarding-instruction findings that supervised, corrected
practice beats unsupervised drill. **Framing note:** no source frames "non-punitive
feedback vs. forced restart" as a typing-specific settled finding; this is a general
motor-learning principle applied to typing, and we cite it as such.

- [Effectiveness of knowledge of results and knowledge of performance in the learning of a skilled motor activity — PMC/NCBI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4905894/) —
  peer-reviewed comparison of informational feedback types in motor skill acquisition.
- [When is knowledge of performance (KP) superior to knowledge of results (KR)? A systematic review — Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1986849) —
  systematic review of feedback types/timing in motor learning.
- [Keyboarding instruction: Comparison of techniques for improved keyboarding skills in elementary students — Journal of Occupational Therapy, Schools, & Early Intervention](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19411243.2018.1512067) —
  direct instruction (with real-time correction) outperforms unsupervised tutorial-only
  practice. *(Full text paywalled; verify exact quotes before excerpting.)*
- [Effects of Initial Touch Keyboarding Speed Achievement of Fifth Graders… — Ertl, ERIC ED499927 (PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED499927.pdf) —
  elementary keyboarding instruction and long-term skill retention
  ([ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED499927)).

## Difficulty philosophy (settings, 2026-07-11)
ADVENTURE (default): no timers, no lives — the entire base game. SURVIVAL (opt-in):
NOVICE / ARCADE / EXPERT tiers — tuned "challenging, never cruel" (expert ≈ 9s for a
4-char pattern), with an arcade-culture mercy mechanic: a 15-streak earns a quarter back.
Game over is warm: "YOU TYPED n LETTERS. THE ARCADE REMEMBERS."

**Naming provenance — corrected.** This section previously claimed the tiers were "named
for 1979 pinball's literal NOVICE/EXPERT switches." The citation pass could not
substantiate that: no manufacturer manual, IPDB entry, or pinball-history source documents
a NOVICE/EXPERT-labeled switch on any 1979-era machine. Real period terminology was
"liberal / normal / difficult" (or liberal/conservative) post positions on EM machines,
and DIP-switch settings (balls per game, replay scores, extra-ball awards) on 1979
solid-state machines like Stern's Trident — none labeled by player skill. The
NOVICE/ARCADE/EXPERT names stay (they're good names), but the pinball-history
justification is **anecdotal — no strong source found**, and the tiers are better
described as "in the spirit of" operator-adjustable arcade difficulty settings.

   Atari 1981, is sometimes claimed to have "novice to expert" ratings — arcade-museum.com's
   DIP sheet shows only Easy/Medium/Hard, so that lead was a dead end too.)

   rationale) — slightly off.** Norvig's corpus: Z is rarest (0.09%), Q second-rarest
