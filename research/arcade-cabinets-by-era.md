# Arcade Cabinet Research: Visual & Technical Reference by Era

Research compiled for Keystroke Kingdom level design reference. Covers iconic arcade cabinets from 1972-1998, organized by era, with visual style, gameplay, controls, and technical details for each.

---

## 1970s: The Dawn of Arcade Gaming

### Pong (1972)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Atari (concept assigned by Nolan Bushnell; designed and built by engineer Allan Alcorn) |
| **Year** | 1972 |
| **Era** | Pre-microprocessor era |
| **Screen Orientation** | Horizontal |
| **Visual Style** | Pure black-and-white. Two rectangular paddles, a square dot (ball), a dotted center line, and simple numeric score digits. No sprites, no color, no background — the purest possible abstraction of a game. |
| **Color Palette** | Monochrome (white on black CRT) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Simulate table tennis. Hit the ball past your opponent's paddle to score. First to 11 points wins. |
| **Input Method** | Rotary knob (paddle controller) — one per player. Analog, continuous movement. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The "bip" and "bop" sound effects generated from the sync generator circuit. The ball accelerating the longer a rally continues. Paddles that cannot quite reach the top of the screen (a hardware limitation kept as a feature). |
| **Technical Characteristics** | No microprocessor — built entirely from transistor-transistor logic (TTL) circuits. Wires soldered onto boards. No software, no ROM, no RAM. Display elements generated directly by discrete logic hardware. The entire game existed as a physical circuit. |

**References**
- [Pong — CHM Revolution, Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/story/183) — institutional record: Alcorn designed and engineered Pong for Atari in 1972 on Bushnell's assignment. **[fetched]**
- [50 Years of Fun With Pong — Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/blog/50-years-of-fun-with-pong/) — corroborates Bushnell assigning Alcorn the build as a "training exercise" and the Andy Capp's Tavern coin-jam story. *(search result only, not fetched)*
- [Pong — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/pong) — institutional arcade record: Bushnell concept, Alcorn build, ~November 1972 release. *(search result only, not fetched)*
- [Original Pong had no code or microprocessor — Hacker News discussion of pong-story.com circuit analysis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31511719) — community-technical corroboration of the pure-TTL (~60–66 chips), no-CPU/no-software claim from circuit-level analysis. *(search result only, not fetched)*

*Note: "no ROM, no RAM" is a sound inference from the well-sourced no-microprocessor/no-software fact, but no source found this pass states it in those literal words — treat as inference, not quotation.*

### Breakout (1976)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Atari (designed by Nolan Bushnell and Steve Bristow; prototype by Steve Wozniak) |
| **Year** | 1976 |
| **Era** | Late 70s, pre-microprocessor |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) |
| **Visual Style** | Monochrome CRT with colored cellophane strips overlaid on the screen to simulate color. Brick rows appear as yellow (bottom), green, orange, and red (top). Black background. Simple geometric shapes only — paddle, ball, rectangular bricks. |
| **Color Palette** | Simulated 4 colors via cellophane overlay on B&W monitor |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Bounce a ball off a paddle into a wall of bricks to destroy them all. Clear two full screens for maximum score. |
| **Input Method** | Rotary knob (paddle controller). Horizontal movement only. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The paddle shrinking to half-width after the ball breaks through the top red row. Ball speed increasing at 4 hits, 12 hits, and upon contacting orange/red bricks. The satisfying cascade of breaking bricks. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Discrete logic (TTL) game — no CPU, no software. Approximately 100 TTL chips in the production version. Wozniak's prototype used only ~45 chips but relied on unconventional "glitch logic" counter-chain tricks that couldn't be reliably mass-manufactured. All visuals generated by hardware logic circuits, not programmable in any way. |

**References**
- [New Insight into Breakout's Origins — Atari](https://atari.com/blogs/atari/new-insight-into-breakout-s-origins) — Atari's own account: Bushnell/Bristow concept, Wozniak's ~45-chip prototype, and Gary Waters' ~100-chip production redesign at Cyan Engineering.
- [Steve Jobs, Breakout Pioneer — The Strong National Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/steve-jobs-breakout-pioneer/) — Corroborates the sub-50-chip prototype and confirms Atari judged it unfit for mass manufacture, hence the production redesign.

*Correction: the prototype chip count is commonly rounded to "44" but the more precise, source-backed figure is ~45 chips; both Atari's own blog and the Strong Museum of Play corroborate this range rather than exactly 44.*

### Space Invaders (1978)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Taito (designed by Tomohiro Nishikado) |
| **Year** | July 1978 (Japan), November 1978 (North America) |
| **Era** | Late 70s — the game that launched the golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) |
| **Visual Style** | Black background with white sprites. Color achieved via strips of transparent colored cellophane over screen regions (orange/green). Later versions used electronically generated color overlays. Alien designs modeled after squids, crabs, and octopuses. Upright cabinets reflected the CRT image via a semi-transparent mirror with a painted starry backdrop behind. |
| **Color Palette** | Monochrome with regional colored overlays (later versions: full electronic color) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Eliminate all aliens in the formation by shooting them before they reach the bottom. Endless waves — each new wave starts lower and moves faster. |
| **Input Method** | Two-way joystick (left/right only) plus a fire button. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The four-note descending bassline that speeds up as fewer aliens remain. The pixelated alien icon (now a universal symbol for gaming). The mystery UFO crossing the top of the screen. Destructible shields. The unintentional speed increase as aliens are destroyed (CPU drawing fewer sprites = faster frame rate). |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Intel 8080 CPU. Bitmapped framebuffer (raster graphics). Monaural sound via analog circuitry + Texas Instruments SN76477 sound chip. Custom hardware designed specifically for this title. CPU limitation: not powerful enough for color graphics or faster enemy movement — the speed-up when fewer aliens remain was an accidental emergent behavior. |

**References**
- [Space Invaders — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders) — Tomohiro Nishikado design credit, Taito 1978 release, and the Intel 8080 frame-render explanation for the alien speed-up (cross-checked against the two sources below; not used alone).
- Tom's Hardware technical retrospective on the Intel 8080 bottleneck ("Space Invaders arcade game ran faster as enemies died due to Intel 8080 bottleneck") — corroborates the hardware-accident explanation from a coder's technical analysis; found via search, page did not fully render on fetch so treat as corroborating rather than primary.
- RedHotCyber technical writeup ("The secret behind Space Invaders' speed: a technical limitation of the hardware") — independent corroboration of the same explanation; found via search, URL returned an error on direct fetch, so verify accessibility before citing as primary.

*Verified: the speed-up is the accepted historical explanation (an unintentional consequence of the 8080 rendering fewer sprites faster, not a deliberate design decision), and Nishikado is documented as having kept the effect once he noticed it because it added tension.*

### Asteroids (1979)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Atari (designed by Lyle Rains and Ed Logg) |
| **Year** | November 1979 |
| **Era** | Late 70s |
| **Screen Orientation** | Horizontal (landscape) |
| **Visual Style** | Vector graphics — bright white lines on black background. No pixels, no sprites, no filled shapes. Pure geometric wireframes. The screen wraps in both axes. Asteroids appear as irregular polygons of various sizes. Ship is a simple triangle. Clean, mathematical, stark. |
| **Color Palette** | Monochrome vector (white/green lines on black) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Shoot and destroy asteroids and flying saucers while avoiding collisions. Large asteroids split into medium, then small pieces. Survive as long as possible for high score. |
| **Input Method** | Five buttons: rotate left, rotate right, thrust, fire, hyperspace (random teleport). No joystick. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The "heartbeat" soundtrack that quickens as gameplay intensifies. The triangular ship. Asteroids fracturing into smaller, faster pieces. The screen-wrap mechanic. 13 hand-wired sound effects (no sound chip). Vector glow — the distinctive bright phosphor bloom of the vector monitor. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | MOS 6502 CPU. Atari QuadraScan / Digital Vector Generator (DVG) — draws geometric lines rather than raster pixels. Resolution is effectively infinite (continuous vector coordinates, not a pixel grid). Maximum 26 asteroids on-screen simultaneously. No sprite hardware — all objects are line segments calculated and drawn by the DVG. |

**References**
- [Asteroids — The Strong National Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/games/asteroids/) — institutional confirmation: Ed Logg as designer, Lyle Rains as concept originator, November 1979 release, vector display. **[fetched]**
- [Asteroids — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/asteroids) — institutional record; lists Wendi Allen (credited historically as Howard Delman) as Project Engineer of the vector display hardware. **[fetched]**
- [Atari Asteroids: Creating a Vector Arcade Classic — The Arcade Blogger](https://arcadeblogger.com/2018/10/24/atari-asteroids-creating-a-vector-arcade-classic/) — corroborates the Rains (concept) / Logg (design and program) split, and the vector hardware adapted from Lunar Lander. **[fetched]**
- [Asteroids (video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids_(video_game)) — MOS 6502 CPU, QuadraScan/DVG vector system, the 13 hand-wired sound effects (no sound chip), and the 26-asteroid on-screen cap (large asteroids at the cap split into one medium piece instead of two to enforce it). Used alongside the museum sources, not alone. **[fetched]**

*Note: the engineer who hand-wired the sound board and built the vector generator is credited on older sources as Howard Delman and on arcade-museum.com's current people page as Wendi Allen (her current name) — use "Wendi Allen" if a hardware credit is ever added to game copy.*

### Galaxian (1979)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Namco |
| **Year** | September 1979 (Japan), February 1980 (North America) |
| **Era** | Late 70s — bridge to the golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) |
| **Visual Style** | The first game with multi-colored sprites — individual pixels within a single sprite could be different colors, a genuine first for the era (color itself was not new: Atari's Color Gotcha, 1973, and Indy 800, 1975, predate it). Scrolling starfield background. Colorful alien formation at top (red, blue, purple flagships with escorts). The player's white Galaxip at the bottom. Rich color compared to the monochrome era it replaced. |
| **Color Palette** | Full RGB color with multi-colored sprites — a major step for 1979 (per-pixel sprite color was the new part, not color itself) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Destroy all aliens in the formation. Enemies break from formation to dive-bomb the player while firing projectiles. Clear the wave to advance. |
| **Input Method** | Two-way joystick (left/right) plus fire button. Single shot only — must wait for projectile to hit or leave screen before firing again. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The scrolling starfield (a first for arcade games). Flagship aliens with escorts offering bonus points when destroyed together. Sound effects inspired by Star Wars space battles. The Galaxian flagship appears as the 6th bonus fruit in Pac-Man. First Namco game composed with a synthesizer. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Pioneering tilemap hardware system (the first to use tile-based rendering). 8x8 pixel tiles, animated multi-color sprites over scrolling background. Reduced processing/memory requirements by up to 64x compared to Space Invaders' bitmap approach. This hardware directly influenced Nintendo's Donkey Kong board and ultimately the Famicom/NES architecture. |

**References**
- [Galaxian — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxian) — September 15, 1979 Japan release (sourced there to 1981 Midway court records) / February 1980 North America; the tilemap-hardware claim (8x8 tiles, up-to-64x memory/processing reduction vs. Space Invaders' framebuffer); influence on the Radar Scope/Donkey Kong boards and Famicom/NES architecture; the Galaxian flagship as Pac-Man's sixth bonus fruit. Cross-checked, not used alone. **[fetched]**
- [What Was The First "True" Color Arcade Video Game? — The Golden Age Arcade Historian (All In Color For A Quarter)](http://allincolorforaquarter.blogspot.com/2012/09/what-was-first-true-color-arcade-video.html) — specialist arcade-history analysis disputing the "first true color" superlative (Atari's Color Gotcha 1973 and Indy 800 1975 predate it) and reframing Galaxian's real first as multi-colored sprites. **[fetched]**
- [Galaxian — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/galaxian) — institutional arcade record for the Namco/Midway release. *(search result only, not fetched)*

*Date discrepancy disclosed: Wikipedia (court-record-sourced) gives Japan September 15, 1979; arcade-history.com gives October 31, 1979 (that page returned a 403 on direct fetch this pass, so it is noted but not cited as read). "September 1979" above follows the stronger-sourced date.*

## Early 1980s: The Golden Age

### Pac-Man (1980)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Namco (designed by Toru Iwatani) |
| **Year** | May 1980 (Japan), October 1980 (USA) |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait), monitor rotated 90 degrees clockwise |
| **Visual Style** | Bright pastel colors on a black background. Blue maze walls. Yellow Pac-Man. Four distinctly colored ghosts (red Blinky, pink Pinky, cyan Inky, orange Clyde) with expressive blue eyes. Colorful fruit bonuses. "Cute, colorful and appealing" — deliberately designed to attract a broader audience beyond the space shooter crowd. |
| **Color Palette** | RGB color display with bright pastels — yellow, blue, red, pink, cyan, orange, white |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Eat all 240 dots in the maze while avoiding four ghosts. Power Pellets temporarily make ghosts vulnerable (they turn blue). Clear the maze to advance. |
| **Input Method** | Four-way joystick (up/down/left/right — no diagonals). |
| **Iconic Elements** | The "wakka-wakka" eating sound. The four ghosts with distinct AI personalities. Power Pellets reversing the chase dynamic. Fruit bonuses (cherry, strawberry, orange, etc.). Intermission cutscenes between levels. The iconic pizza-with-a-slice-missing character design. The kill screen at Level 256. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Z80A CPU at 3.072 MHz. 16 KB ROM, 3 KB SRAM, 8 KB character ROM. Sprites: 16x16 pixels, 4 bits per pixel (one color for transparency). 6 usable sprites (numbered 1-6; sprites 0 and 7 reserved for screen timing). Two custom ICs (sync bus controller + video RAM addresser). Sprite data written during horizontal blanking. |

**References**
- [HISTORY — The Official Site for PAC-MAN (pacman.com)](https://pacman.com/en/history/) — Bandai Namco's own history page: confirms Toru Iwatani as designer, the nine-person dev team, May 1980 Japan test, July 1980 Japan release.
- [Tōru Iwatani — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dru_Iwatani) — Biographical corroboration (design philosophy, prior Namco titles); used alongside the official Namco page, not alone.

### Donkey Kong (1981)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Nintendo (designed by Shigeru Miyamoto) |
| **Year** | July 1981 (Japan), October 1981 (North America) |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) — four single-screen stages |
| **Visual Style** | Colorful platformer with construction site theme. Red-capped Mario (originally "Jumpman"), brown ape, pink-dressed Pauline. Steel girders, ladders, barrels, flames. Each stage represents 25 meters of height (25m, 50m, 75m, 100m). Derived from Radar Scope hardware — similar visual capability to Galaxian. |
| **Color Palette** | Multi-color sprites on dark backgrounds — reds, browns, blues, pinks |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Climb the construction site to rescue Pauline from Donkey Kong. Jump over obstacles (barrels, flames), use hammers, and ultimately remove rivets in the final stage to send DK falling. |
| **Input Method** | Four-way joystick plus one jump button. |
| **Iconic Elements** | Guinness World Records credits Donkey Kong as the first video game to tell "a complete story, with beginning and end," delivered through cutscenes. The Dragnet-inspired opening music. The heart that appears between Mario and Pauline (then breaks). Pauline's "HELP!" speech balloon. The origin of Mario — mustache (implies a mouth), cap (obviates hair animation). Rolling barrels as the primary obstacle. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Built on restructured Radar Scope boards (Galaxian-derived hardware). 128 foreground sprites at 16x16 pixels, 256 background tiles at 8x8 pixels. Mario and moving objects use single sprites; Pauline uses 2 sprites; Donkey Kong uses 6 sprites. Approximately 20 KB of total content. |

**References**
- [First use of cutscenes to tell a story in a videogame — Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-use-of-cutscenes-to-tell-a-story-in-a-video-game) — Official GWR record page (sourced to GWR's *Video Gamer's Edition 2008*); confirms Miyamoto as designer, July 1981 Nintendo release, and the precise "complete story...told within a video game" wording used above.
- [Donkey Kong (1981 video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_(1981_video_game)) — Corroborates Miyamoto's role, the Radar Scope cabinet reuse, and design inspirations (Popeye, King Kong, Beauty and the Beast). Used alongside GWR, not alone.

*Correction: softened "first arcade game with a complete narrative" to the precise, GWR-sourced framing — DK is documented as the first to combine a complete story with cutscene delivery, not simply "the first game with a narrative" in an unqualified sense (earlier games had told stories or used scene-setting separately).*

### Galaga (1981)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Namco |
| **Year** | September 1981 (Japan), November 1981 (North America) |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) — fixed shooter |
| **Visual Style** | Colorful sprites against a black starfield. Distinctive insectoid alien designs in formation. Bright primary colors — red boss aliens, blue/yellow drones, white player ship. More graphically rich than Galaxian, with smoother animation and more detailed sprite work. Stage emblems at bottom-right track progress. |
| **Color Palette** | Multi-color on black — vibrant reds, blues, yellows, whites, greens |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Destroy all aliens in each wave. Aliens fly in from the top/sides into formation, then dive-attack. Clear all enemies to advance. Challenging Stages (bonus rounds) appear periodically. |
| **Input Method** | Two-way joystick (left/right) plus fire button. Can fire two shots on screen simultaneously (unlike Galaxian's single shot). |
| **Iconic Elements** | The Boss Galaga tractor beam that captures your ship. Rescuing your captured ship to form a dual-fighter (double firepower, larger hitbox). Challenging Stages with preset attack patterns and no enemy fire. The satisfying sound effects (later immortalized in a Hallmark ornament). The kill screen at Stage 255 (8-bit integer overflow). |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Namco Galaga arcade system board (successor to Galaxian board). 16x16 pixel sprites with limited on-screen sprite count. Developers worked around sprite limits by combining ship + bullet into a single 16x16 sprite to save sprite slots. Same board powered Bosconian and Dig Dug. |

**References**
- [history — Galaga Web, BANDAI NAMCO Entertainment](https://galaga.com/en/history/galaga.php) — Official Namco source confirming 1981 release and status as successor to Galaxian.
- [Galaga — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaga) — Corroborates the Galaxian-board-to-Galaga-board hardware transition and shared board lineage with Bosconian/Dig Dug. Used alongside the official Namco page, not alone.

### Centipede (1981)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Atari (designed by Dona Bailey and Ed Logg) |
| **Year** | August 1981 |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) |
| **Visual Style** | Distinctive pastel color palette — deliberate choice by designer Dona Bailey to make the game "visually arresting" and different from the dark, neon-heavy games of the era. Mushroom garden in soft greens and pinks. Centipede segments in contrasting colors. The Bug Blaster (player) at the bottom. Fleas, spiders, and scorpions add visual variety. Feels softer and more organic than contemporaries. |
| **Color Palette** | Pastel palette — greens, pinks, purples, soft yellows (unique for the era) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Shoot all segments of a descending centipede that weaves through a mushroom field. Mushrooms deflect the centipede's path. Shooting a middle segment splits it into two independent centipedes. Also defeat fleas, spiders, and scorpions. |
| **Input Method** | Trackball (primary, distinctive) plus fire button. The trackball allowed smooth, fast movement in all directions within the lower portion of the screen. One of few golden age games to use a trackball. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The segmented centipede splitting when shot in the middle. The mushroom field growing denser over time. Scorpions poisoning mushrooms (causing centipedes to dive straight down). The trackball control giving uniquely fluid movement. The approachable, colorful aesthetic that attracted non-traditional arcade players. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Custom Atari arcade hardware. Multiple independent autonomous sprites (each centipede segment tracks independently). Destructible/persistent mushroom field objects that accumulate between lives. The design supported many simultaneous on-screen elements without slowdown. |

**References**
- [Centipede — The Strong National Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/games/centipede/) — institutional confirmation of the deliberately pastel palette ("to make the game compelling and look appealing in a dark arcade") and the trackball's broad-audience appeal. **[fetched]**
- [Centipede — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/centipede) — institutional record crediting Dona Bailey (Co-Developer and Programmer) and Ed Logg (Co-Developer); optical trackball + fire button confirmed. Its "1980" metadata field reflects the development-start year, not release. **[fetched]**
- [Centipede (video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centipede_(video_game)) — August 1981 release; Bailey's own quote on choosing pastels ("I really wanted it to look different, to be visually arresting"); the Logg-design/Bailey-programming split. **[fetched]**
- [Dona Bailey — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dona_Bailey) — biographical corroboration of her role as one of the first female game designers at Atari. *(search result only, not fetched)*

*Dating note: development began July 1980 (the source of arcade-museum.com's "1980" field); the arcade street release was August 1981 as stated above.*

### Frogger (1981)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Konami (published in North America by Sega/Gremlin — often miscredited to Sega alone) |
| **Year** | August 1981 (Japan), September 1981 (North America) |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) — top-down perspective |
| **Visual Style** | Brightly colored top-down view. Screen divided into distinct horizontal lanes: road (gray with vehicles), median strip (green), river (blue with logs/turtles/alligators), frog homes at top. Simple but readable sprites — yellow taxis, purple cars, green logs, turtles. The green frog protagonist. Clear lane-based visual hierarchy. |
| **Color Palette** | Bright multi-color — greens, blues, grays, yellows, purples on black surround |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Guide five frogs from the bottom to five home slots at the top. Cross a busy road (dodging vehicles) then navigate a river (jumping on logs, turtles, and alligators). All within a 30-second timer per frog. |
| **Input Method** | Four-way joystick. Each tap moves the frog exactly one space — no continuous movement. Discrete, hop-based navigation. |
| **Iconic Elements** | "The arcade game with the most ways to die" — vehicles, drowning, alligator mouths, snakes, diving turtles, running out of time, jumping into occupied homes. The skull-and-crossbones death marker. Opening tune: Japanese children's song "Inu no Omawarisan." The American version plays "Yankee Doodle" upon successfully homing a frog. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Z80 processor architecture. Score maximum: 99,990 (rolls over). DIP-switch configurable starting lives (3, 5, or 7). 30-second timer per frog displayed as 60 ticks. Multiple independently-moving lane objects all scrolling at different speeds simultaneously. |

**References**
- [Frogger — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/frogger) — institutional record: developer "Konami (Japan)," released in the US by Sega/Gremlin; its trivia section explicitly notes "Although Frogger is credited to Sega it was originally developed by Konami." **[fetched]**

### Defender (1981)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Williams Electronics (designed by Eugene Jarvis) |
| **Year** | February 1981 (Japan), March 1981 (North America) |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Horizontal (landscape) — wider than standard (320x256 vs. industry standard 256x256) |
| **Visual Style** | Side-scrolling shooter with bright, detailed sprites against a dark star-filled background. Varied sprite colors for different enemy types. Particle-effect explosions (unique for the era). A landscape depicted as a thin pixel-wide line at the bottom. Minimap at screen top showing the full world. Flashing, intense, fast-moving. |
| **Color Palette** | 16 colors per pixel — bright neons against black (deliberately more colorful than competitors) |
| **Gameplay Objective** | Destroy alien invaders while protecting astronauts on the planet surface from abduction. If Landers carry humans to the top, they become deadly Mutants. Lose all astronauts and the planet explodes. |
| **Input Method** | Two-way joystick (up/down only) plus five buttons (thrust, reverse, fire, smart bomb, hyperspace). Notoriously complex control scheme — left hand on joystick, right hand on buttons. |
| **Iconic Elements** | The minimap (an innovation for arcade games). Unique particle-effect explosions generated algorithmically (never the same twice). Incredible difficulty. The reverse button (allowing instant direction change). Sound: amplified monaural audio with distinctive enemy and explosion effects. The panic of watching a Lander carry away a human. |
| **Technical Characteristics** | Motorola 6809 CPU (gameplay/graphics) + Motorola 6800 (audio). Resolution: 320x256 pixels (wider than standard). 16-color display. Software-driven animation (not hardware sprites) — allowed more on-screen objects at lower cost. All programmed in assembly language. 3x AA battery backup for high scores. |

**References**
- [Defender — Museum of the Game (arcade-museum.com/KLOV)](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/defender--williams) — Williams Electronics, 1981; dual-processor architecture (Motorola 6809 for gameplay/graphics, 6800 for sound). **[fetched]**
- [Defender — The Strong National Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/games/defender/) — Eugene Jarvis-led design team (with Larry DeMar, Sam Dicker, Paul Dussault); the minimap innovation; particle-style explosion effects; two-way joystick + five-button controls. **[fetched]**
- [Defender (1981 video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defender_(1981_video_game)) — February 1981 (Japan) / March 1981 (North America) timing, 320x256 resolution, 16-color display, Sam Dicker's algorithmically-generated particle explosions, and the 3x AA battery backup. **[fetched]**

### Q*bert (1982)

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Developer** | Gottlieb (programmed and designed by Warren Davis, from an original character/cube-pattern concept by artist Jeff Lee) |
| **Year** | October 1982 |
| **Era** | Early golden age |
| **Screen Orientation** | Vertical (portrait) |
| **Visual Style** | Isometric pseudo-3D perspective — a 2D game rendered in axonometric projection. Black background with a multicolored pyramid of 28 cubes viewed from above-right.
