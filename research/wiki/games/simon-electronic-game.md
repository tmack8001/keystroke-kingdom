Simon (**1978**, Milton Bradley) is the round four-colour electronic memory game invented by
**Ralph H. Baer and Howard J. Morrison** at Marvin Glass and Associates, launched at **Studio
54** in New York. It flashes a growing sequence of lights, each with its own musical tone, and
asks the player to play it back. Wikipedia's music-video-game article calls it **"the earliest
example of a rhythm game."** Baer built it from Atari's [[touch-me-atari]] (1974) specifically
to make the feedback *musical and pleasant* rather than harsh — which is, almost word for word,
Keystroke Kingdom's own audio design brief.

## In this game

The second station of a proposed music/dance act ([[rhythm-games-by-era]] §2), and its
**1978 date sits one year before the arcade this whole game is set in**. Two ideas here are
directly transplantable:

1. **Call-and-response with a built-in wait.** Simon performs, then **does nothing at all**
   until the player answers. A pause is part of the form, not a concession to it — which is the
   only known way to reconcile a rhythm mechanic with the sacred contract that *the letter stays
   on screen and waits* ([[call-and-response]], [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §5).
2. **A tone per button.** Simon's four buttons sound an **A-major triad in second inversion**,
   so playing correctly *is itself music*. A keyboard has twenty times as many buttons; giving
   keys pleasant pitches so a correctly-typed word makes a pleasant phrase is a 1978-vintage
   idea that interrupts nothing.

**Punishment to strip (One Rule):** **one wrong press ends the game**, and there is a response
time limit. This is the harshest single-mistake rule in the whole genre. Reframe: a wrong press
simply **replays the phrase**; nothing ends.

**What to draw:** the **round four-colour handheld toy**, on a shelf or counter. Simon was never
an arcade cabinet — drawing one would break the "draw a real thing" rule
([[racing-games-by-era]] §17). Its cabinet-shaped ancestor is [[touch-me-atari]].

## The facts

- **Inventors:** Ralph H. Baer and Howard J. Morrison, working for **Marvin Glass and
  Associates**. **Manufacturer:** Milton Bradley (Hasbro acquired MB in the 1980s and still
  makes variants).
- **Launch: 1978, at Studio 54, New York City**; an immediate hit that holiday season.
- **Play:** the unit generates sequences of coloured lights and tones; the player repeats them;
  each round adds a step. A wrong press, or exceeding the time limit, ends the game.
- **The tones:** the original sounded an **A-major triad in second inversion**, deliberately
  resembling a trumpet fanfare — red (upper right) **A**, blue (lower right) **E**, yellow
  (lower left) **C♯**, green (upper left) **E an octave lower**. Some 1978 units used a
  **B♭-minor triad** instead.
- **Origin:** Baer encountered Atari's [[touch-me-atari]] and criticised it — *"Terrible
  execution. Visually boring. Miserable, rasping sounds."* Simon was his corrected version.
  ⚠️ **The year of that encounter is contradictory across two sources fetched the same day:
  1976 (Simon's article) vs 1977 (Touch Me's article). Write "the mid-1970s."**
- **Status in the genre:** Wikipedia's *Music video game* article states Simon "is considered
  the earliest example of a rhythm game," ahead of [[dance-aerobics]] (1987) and
  [[parappa-the-rapper]] (1996).
- **Not** in The Strong's National Toy Hall of Fame as of the 2025 inductee list fetched this
  session — do not claim an induction.
