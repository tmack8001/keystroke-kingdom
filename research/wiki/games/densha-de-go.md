Densha de Go! (**Taito**, arcade **March 1997**, Japan; designer **Akira Saito**) is a train-driving
simulator whose arcade cabinet reproduces a Japanese commuter train's cab — **brake handle and
throttle levers**, brake-pressure indicators, door-interlock lights — and whose gameplay is
**precision, not speed**: obey the signals, obey the speed limits, and stop *exactly* at the mark
on the platform, on time. It filled Japanese arcades with commuting salarymen who had never played
a video game.

## In this game

**A genuine oddity worth knowing about, but recommended as a footnote rather than a station.**
Its virtue is that it is the one vehicle game in this whole survey where **the goal is accuracy
rather than velocity** — which makes it thematically closer to Act 6 (Text Worlds, typing without
speed) than to Act 7. If Act 7 is *"the faster and more accurate your typing, the faster your
vehicle,"* Densha de Go! is the **accuracy half made literal**, and a spec lane might want it
exactly for that reason.

Against it: the arcade cabinet's whole identity is a **physical master controller**, not a
keyboard; the cultural context (Japanese commuter rail timetabling) needs more explaining to a
Western child than it repays; and it adds no rendering step the act does not already have.

## The facts

- **Developer/publisher:** Taito; designer **Akira Saito**. **Arcade release March 1997 in Japan.**
  **Date correction:** it is frequently dated **1996** — that is the year it was *demonstrated*, at
  the **1996 Amusement Machine Show** in Tokyo. Shown 1996, **shipped March 1997**. (Created-vs-
  shipped again.)
- **Controls:** the arcade cabinet features a **brake handle and throttle levers** mimicking real
  train controls, and is modelled on the cab of a **205 series EMU**, down to brake-pressure
  indicators and door-interlock lights. Home versions shipped a **special controller** copying the
  arcade setup.
- **Gameplay:** guide a Japanese passenger train along one of **four commuter routes** to its
  destination, obeying speed restrictions and signals and **stopping precisely at platforms**.
- **Punishment mechanics — and they are unusual.** The game runs on a **bonus-second** economy:
  accurate stops and on-time arrivals **add** seconds; speeding and overshooting **deduct** them.
  *"When all of the bonus seconds are exhausted, the game ends."* So the timer is a **currency you
  earn by being careful**, not a countdown you merely survive — structurally much closer to
  "typing adds road" than any racing game in this survey.
- **Commercial:** the PlayStation port (**December 1997**) **sold over one million copies**,
  earning Sony's Platinum Prize. Further ports: Windows (April 1999), Game Boy Color (2000),
  WonderSwan (2000). Development was famously cheap and quick — about four months, reusing
  hardware from a sticker printer.
