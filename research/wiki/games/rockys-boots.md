Rocky's Boots (The Learning Company, 1982) is the game in which **[[warren-robinett|Warren
Robinett]] and Leslie Grimm taught children digital logic by letting them wire it up**. The
player drags AND, OR and NOT gates and flip-flops onto a workbench to build a circuit that
drives a mechanical boot, which kicks the objects matching the circuit's criteria off a
conveyor belt. It won Software of the Year, a Parents' Choice award and a Software
Publishers Association Gold, and is documented as "one of the first educational software
products for personal computers to successfully use an interactive graphical simulation as a
learning environment."

## In this game

This page exists because of a connection the project had not noticed: the author of Keystroke
Kingdom's most beloved easter egg is also the author of the best argument for the game's
proposed **learn-to-program act** (`docs/ROADMAP.md` §2).

Typing `adventure` on the title screen answers `WARREN ROBINETT WAS HERE. 1979.`
([[easter-egg-culture]], `.claude/rules/easter-eggs.md`). Three years after hiding that name,
the same programmer co-founded [[the-learning-company]] and shipped a game where a
seven-year-old builds a working logic circuit. If this game ever adds a wing about
programming, Rocky's Boots is its historical anchor — and the anchor is already standing in
our easter-egg layer.

Design lessons worth carrying, if that act is ever built:
- **The machine the child builds actually runs.** The boot kicks or it doesn't. That is
  honest, immediate, non-verbal feedback — and crucially it is *not a score*.
- **A wrong circuit is a circuit you can see.** The failure is legible and fixable on the
  same screen, which is the spirit of our wrong-keypress rule (the letter stays and waits;
  see [[error-feedback-pedagogy]]).
- **It is a simulation, not a quiz** — the exception to the era's drill-and-practice default
  documented in [[educational-software]].

Its sequel, *Robot Odyssey*, extends the same idea into robot programming. Note the tonal
warning for a kid-facing act: Robot Odyssey is famously, punishingly hard — the opposite of
the One Rule. Take Rocky's Boots's openness, not its sequel's difficulty.

## The facts

- **Designers:** Warren Robinett and Leslie Grimm (Grimm also created [[reader-rabbit]]).
- **Publisher:** [[the-learning-company]]. **Release year: 1982.**
- **Platforms:** Apple II, TRS-80 Color Computer, Commodore 64, IBM PC and IBM PCjr.
- **What it taught:** digital logic fundamentals — a review is quoted as saying it conveyed
  "circuit design and Boolean logic to children" and taught "the fundamentals of digital
  computer logic from the ground up."
- **Gameplay:** the player uses logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) and flip-flops to design circuits
  controlling a mechanical boot that kicks objects off a conveyor belt based on specified
  criteria.
- **Historical significance:** "one of the first educational software products for personal
  computers to successfully use an interactive graphical simulation as a learning
  environment."
- **Awards:** Software of the Year, *Learning* magazine (1983); *Parents' Choice* award
  (1983); *InfoWorld* runner-up (1982); Software Publishers Association Gold Award
  (100,000+ copies sold).
- **Sequel:** *Robot Odyssey*, also by Robinett at The Learning Company, for the Apple II.
- **Unverified this session:** Robot Odyssey's release year and its reputation for extreme
  difficulty (widely repeated, not sourced here — the tonal warning above is offered as
  project judgement, not as a cited claim). Robinett's own design writing on Rocky's Boots
  could not be retrieved; warrenrobinett.com failed TLS handshake and was dropped.
