The Oregon Trail was written on **December 3, 1971** by three Carleton College student
teachers — **Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger** — for an HP 2100
minicomputer accessed through teleprinter terminals. It had no graphics; it printed on paper.
Rawitsch carried the code to MECC in 1974, MECC put it on its statewide time-sharing network
in 1975, and an Apple II version followed in 1980. The Strong National Museum of Play, which
inducted it into the World Video Game Hall of Fame in 2016, calls it "the longest-published,
most successful educational game of all time."

Its relevance to Keystroke Kingdom is specific and unglamorous: **Oregon Trail is proof that

worked — the same claim this game makes when Level 1 is deliberately rendered as 1976
black-and-white rectangles (`.claude/rules/product.md` §5, "Graphic Quality Grows With the

rather than drilled*, the distinction at the heart of [[educational-software]]. Its text-only
original is a direct cousin of Act 6's typed text worlds.

## The facts

- **Created December 3, 1971** by Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger, all
  Carleton College students working as student teachers. The Strong: "Three student teachers,
  Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann, and Paul Dillenberger, created *The Oregon Trail* in 1971 to
  help Minnesota schoolchildren learn American History."
- **Built fast, on paper first:** "The trio then spent the weekend designing and coding the
  game on paper" before implementing it **in two weeks** in HP Time-Shared BASIC.
- **Original hardware:** an HP 2100 minicomputer connected via teleprinter terminals — The
  Strong describes it as "programmed on a primitive teletype printer." No screen, no
  graphics.
- **MECC route:** Rawitsch joined [[mecc|MECC]] in 1974 and "spent the 1974 Thanksgiving
  weekend copying and adjusting the printed BASIC source code into the system." MECC placed
  it on its time-sharing network in **1975**, "where it could be accessed by schools across
  Minnesota." That version "was the most popular software in the system for Minnesota schools
  for five years, drawing thousands of players monthly."
- **Apple II version:** adapted by **John Cook**, included as *Oregon* in MECC's Elementary
  series in **1980**, "distributed to Minnesota schools for free and for profit to schools
  outside of the state."
- **Scale:** The Strong reports over **65 million copies** sold. Wikipedia's MECC article
  notes Oregon Trail generated about **one-third of MECC's $30 million revenue by 1995**.
- **Institutional recognition:** inducted into the **World Video Game Hall of Fame in 2016**;
  The Strong credits it with pioneering "a blend of learning and play that showcases the
  valuable contribution games can make to education."
- **Designer note:** R. Philip Bouchard, who designed [[number-munchers|Number Munchers]]
  (1986), also designed The Oregon Trail series — a single designer behind two of MECC's
  landmarks.
- **Precision caution — three different "first" years circulate.** 1971 is when it was
  written; 1975 is when MECC put it on the statewide network; 1980 is the Apple II release
  most adults actually remember playing. State which one you mean (this is the
  created-vs-shipped trap from the arcade-historian brief).
