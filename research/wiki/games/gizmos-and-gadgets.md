Super Solvers: Gizmos & Gadgets! (The Learning Company, 1993) is a science game for ages
7–12 in which the child solves physics puzzles to win vehicle parts, **builds a vehicle out
of the parts they won**, and then races it against the series villain Morty Maxwell. Fifteen
races across three categories; lose one and you go back and build a better machine. Its
subject is introductory mechanics — simple machines, magnets, basic electronics and forms of
energy — but its actual lesson is the engineering loop: hypothesise, build, test, revise.

## In this game

The second of two titles Trevor named as childhood favourites to place "somewhere / somehow"
(the other is [[stickybear]]), with the explicit constraint: *"Of course not in the raceway."*

That constraint is worth taking seriously, because **Gizmos & Gadgets is, mechanically, a
racing game** — Wikipedia files it under Platform, Puzzle and Racing, and its win condition
is fifteen races. But the racing is not the skill being taught; it is the *test bench*. The
child never gets faster at racing, they get better at building. That distinction is exactly
why it does not belong in Act 7 (the Speedway, where speed itself is the skill — see
[[typing-as-racing]]) and why it *does* rhyme with the proposed learn-to-program act in
`docs/ROADMAP.md` §2. Its true sibling in this wiki is [[rockys-boots]] — the other
[[the-learning-company|Learning Company]] title where a child assembles a working machine
and then watches it succeed or fail on its own terms.

**Its deepest relevance to the One Rule:** in Gizmos & Gadgets, losing a race is not a
punishment, it is *information* — the game hands the loss back as a reason to return to the
warehouse. That is the same emotional contract as our wrong-keypress behaviour (the letter
stays and waits) stated in a different medium. See [[educational-software]] for the full
lineage argument and [[error-feedback-pedagogy]] for what actually grounds the One Rule.

## The facts

- **Full title:** *Super Solvers: Gizmos & Gadgets!* — part of The Learning Company's
  **Super Solvers** series (the series line is documented as targeting ages 7–12, alongside
  [[reader-rabbit|Reader Rabbit]] for ages 2–8 and Treasure Mountain for 5–9).
- **Developer and publisher:** [[the-learning-company|The Learning Company]].
- **Release:** **1993** (North America), on **MS-DOS and Macintosh**. A later CD release
  added Windows 95 compatibility; Internet Archive holds a **1997** re-release for Windows
  3.1 / Windows 95 / Macintosh and a **1998** Windows CD version, plus a cross-platform
  CD-ROM (Windows v1.1 / Macintosh v1.01).
- **Audience and subject:** ages 7–12; "introductory mechanics, namely simple machines,
  magnets, basic electronics, and forms of energy." Wikipedia's fuller concept list adds
  balance, electricity, energy types, force, gears and magnetics.
- **Gameplay loop:** the player navigates warehouse mazes at the Shady Glen Technology
  Center, solving physics-based puzzles to collect vehicle parts, then assembles a vehicle
  and races it. "The objective of the game is to win 15 races in different vehicles against
  Morty Maxwell at the Shady Glen Technology Center."
- **Three race categories, five races each:** automotive, alternative energy, and aircraft.
  Puzzle difficulty increases as the player advances.
- **The build genuinely matters:** design choices change race outcomes — the game teaches,
  for example, that "a rounded nose on a car is more aerodynamic and faster than a flat
  nose."
- **Antagonists:** Morty Maxwell is the recurring Super Solvers villain and the player's
  race opponent; his henchmen, the **Cyberchimps**, steal parts while the player is
  navigating the warehouses.
- **Unverified this session:** sales figures, critical reception, designer credits, and
  whether the game had any timed-failure state. Nothing above depends on these. Note in
  particular that **no source found confirms the game is non-punitive** — the "losing is
  information" reading above is a design *interpretation* of the documented rebuild loop,
  not a sourced claim about the game's feedback behaviour.
