Tetris was created by **Alexey Pajitnov** in 1984 in the USSR — on an Elektronika 60 at the
Dorodnitsyn Computing Centre, with zero Nintendo involvement — five years before Nintendo's
first Tetris release, the 1989 Game Boy version, arrived via a separate license with
Bullet-Proof Software.

## In this game

Tetris is the **Level 8 cabinet**, teaching T and Y. **Important correction carried onto
this page:** `.claude/rules/levels.md` (Level 8) and `CLAUDE.md`'s level table credit Tetris
as "Pajitnov / Nintendo, 1984," which conflates two unrelated facts into one misleading
citation. The correct framing is two dated facts: Pajitnov created the original in 1984
(USSR, no Nintendo involvement); Nintendo's Game Boy version shipped in 1989, five years
later, via a license with Bullet-Proof Software. This has been flagged for the doc owner in
both hub research files but not edited in `levels.md` or `CLAUDE.md` per task instructions.
In-game, Tetris's clean geometric minimalism is deliberately used as a "palette cleanser"
after [[dragons-lair]]'s cinematic lavishness.

## The facts

- Original creation: 1984, by Alexey Pajitnov, on an Elektronika 60 at the Dorodnitsyn
  Computing Centre, USSR Academy of Sciences.
- Arcade versions (the versions the "1988 arcade" cabinet entry describes): Sega's arcade
  version, Sega System 16 hardware, December 1988 (undisputed across sources). Atari
  Games' cabinet (published as Tengen) reads "1988" on its title screen/copyright, but the
  documented street release was February 1989.
- Nintendo's first and only Tetris release in this era: the Game Boy version — Japan June
  14, 1989; North America July 31, 1989; Europe September 28, 1990 — via license with
  Bullet-Proof Software, not a Nintendo-original creation.
- **The 1984 original had NO GRAPHICS AT ALL.** *"Because the Elektronika 60 had no graphical
  interface, Pajitnov modeled the field and pieces using spaces and brackets"* — the well and
  every tetromino were **typed characters** on a monochrome terminal. He wrote it in **Pascal
  in about three weeks**, then spent over two months porting it to the IBM PC in Turbo Pascal
  with Dmitry Pavlovsky and Vadim Gerasimov. The Elektronika 60 was **a Russian clone of the
  PDP-11**. *(This is the single most on-theme fact in the wiki for this project: the level
  that teaches T and Y sits on a machine where the game itself was made of typed characters —
  and it is why the Level 8 station's screen is drawn as brackets, not coloured blocks.
  `js/ui/cabinet-art.js` `drawStationScreen`.)*
- Visual style (from the IBM PC port onward): minimalist geometric design — a rectangular well/matrix with 7 distinct
  tetromino shapes (I, O, T, S, Z, J, L), each a distinct color. No characters, no
  narrative.
- Iconic: the "Korobeiniki" Russian folk theme became globally iconic via Nintendo's 1989
  Game Boy version; the satisfaction of a four-line clear ("a Tetris").
- The console/handheld rights legal battle (Mirrorsoft/Tengen/Atari Games vs.
  Nintendo/BPS, resolved via Nintendo securing the Game Boy rights) shaped the industry's
  approach to licensing.
