The Falcon series — **Falcon** (Sphere / Spectrum HoloByte, **1987**, Mac and MS-DOS) through
**Falcon 3.0** (**1991**) — is the benchmark of the combat flight-sim era: a simulated F-16 with
fully modelled electronic displays and radar, AI opponents that fly like real pilots, a **campaign
engine** running fictional wars, and hardware demands (a **math coprocessor** for the high-fidelity
flight model) that made it a machine-buying decision. It is also the best single piece of evidence
for [[keyboard-as-cockpit]] — and the clearest example of why a **children's** flight act should
not go this way.

## In this game

**Documented as history; recommended AGAINST as a cabinet.** The full reasoning is in
`research/flight-simulators-by-era.md` §6, but in short: the register is wrong. Being shot down
and shooting others down is not a thing this game does, and the One Rule ("stop the player's flow
with joy, never with punishment") is not the only objection — a *mission failure* structure is the
punishment mechanic the whole act would have to strip.

**What it IS good for:** Falcon 3.0's in-box **Quick Reference Card** is the strongest surviving
proof that flight sims were keyboard-operated — ~**80–90 key commands across ~14 categories**. Use
the card as an *artifact*, and put the flying in [[chuck-yeagers-advanced-flight-trainer]],
[[pilotwings]] or [[flight-unlimited]] instead.

## The facts

- **Falcon (1987):** developed by **Sphere, Inc.**, published by **Spectrum HoloByte**; Macintosh
  and MS-DOS 1987, with Rowan Software ports to Atari ST (1988) and Amiga (1989), plus PC-98
  (1988), Commodore CDTV and TurboGrafx-16 (1992). **12 missions**; fully simulated electronic
  displays and radar with **4 HUD styles**; AI enemies that barrel-roll and scissor; one of the
  earliest games offering **head-to-head play by linking two machines**. An F-16 pilot of the
  **474th Tactical Fighter Wing** gave it good marks for accuracy; *Computer Gaming World* called
  it *"one of the most detailed and accurate flight simulators for the microcomputer market."*
  Won 1987 Software Publishers Association awards and the 1989 Golden Joystick for Best 16-bit
  Simulation Game.
- **Falcon 3.0 (1991):** **Sphere Inc.**, published by **Spectrum HoloByte** (North America) and
  **MicroProse** (Europe); MS-DOS; **VGA** graphics praised by a serving F-16 pilot. It **required
  a math coprocessor** for the high-fidelity flight mode and was *"virtually unplayable on
  computers slower than a 386"* — a 33 MHz 486 was the recommended machine. Its **campaign engine**
  simulated fictional wars in **Iraq, Israel and Panama** with finite, periodically replenished
  supplies of pilots, aircraft and weapons, plus a **Red Flag** training-exercise simulation.
  *Computer Gaming World* credited it with the **first truly realistic flight model** for jet
  aircraft in a commercial game.
- **The Quick Reference Card** (primary artifact, Internet Archive): ~80–90 key commands across
  map control, stick, throttle/afterburner, weapon selection, radar, **13 view keys**,
  zoom/rotation, countermeasures, flight controls (autopilot, brakes, flaps, gear, ejection),
  AVTR/replay, stores, **14 wingman-order keys**, comms and miscellaneous.
- **Rating context for the genre:** modern combat flight sims rate higher than civilian ones —
  **Ace Combat 7: Skies Unknown** is **ESRB T** for Language, Mild Blood and Violence (the ESRB
  summary describes aerial dogfights, missiles, machine guns and bombs, and a cutscene with blood
  on a pilot's helmet). By contrast **Microsoft Combat Flight Simulator** is **ESRB E with
  "Animated Violence."** So the argument against combat here is **not** a ratings argument — it is
  a register argument. Say so honestly.
