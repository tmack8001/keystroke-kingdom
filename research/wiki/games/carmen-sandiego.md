Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego? (Brøderbund, **April 23, 1985**) put the player in the
ACME Detective Agency chasing V.I.L.E. thieves across thirty countries, answering geography
questions by consulting a **real printed almanac that came in the box**. The detail that
makes it the most interesting title in [[educational-software]] is this: it "was not intended
as an educational game at release." It became the defining educational game of its generation
by accident, in schools, because children wanted to play it.

## In this game

Two things here matter to Keystroke Kingdom.

**First, the almanac.** The game shipped with *The World Almanac and Book of Facts* and
required the player to look things up in it — which doubled as copy protection. A commercial
necessity was turned into the best part of the design: the child leaves the screen, opens a
book, and comes back. Keystroke Kingdom's history cards do a softer version of the same move
(the child types a word and receives a real fact about the world outside the game), and Act
6's text worlds do the harder one. If a "look it up" mechanic is ever built here, this is the
precedent.

**Second, the accident.** The best-remembered educational game of the 1980s was not designed
as educational software. That is a genuine caution against the assumption that teaching
intent produces loved products — the causation ran the other way. Compare [[number-munchers]],
which *was* designed to teach and is remembered as school.

Brøderbund is also the publisher of [[myst]], our Level 13 cabinet — the same house made the
game a child looked up countries for and the game that redefined what a CD-ROM could look
like.

## The facts

- **Developer and publisher:** Brøderbund. **Released April 23, 1985.**
- **Original platforms included Apple II and Commodore 64**, with an unusually long port
  list over the following decade: Amstrad CPC, Apple IIGS, Amiga, Atari ST, Classic Mac OS,
  MS-DOS, FM Towns, Master System, TRS-80 Color Computer, TurboGrafx-16, Genesis and
  Super NES.
- **Gameplay:** the player is an ACME Detective Agency recruit tracking V.I.L.E. criminals
  across **30 countries**, succeeding by "using their knowledge of geography (aided by the
  *Almanac*) to question witnesses or investigate clues."
- **The almanac was a real book in the box:** the game was "distributed with *The World
  Almanac and Book of Facts*, published by Pharos Books." Players consulted the printed
  reference to answer the geography questions required for rank promotions — **which
  functioned as copy protection.**
- **Not designed as educational software:** the game "was not intended as an educational game
  at release," but became commercially successful in schools.
- **Scale:** by 1995, "over four million copies of the game had been sold."
- **Publisher context:** Brøderbund was founded in 1980 by brothers **Doug and Gary
  Carlston** (Doug a lawyer, Gary a former college Swedish teacher); their sister Cathy joined
  a year later. Carmen Sandiego was the company's **first internally developed product** and
  later a major revenue driver. Brøderbund also published *The Print Shop* (33% of revenue at
  its 1991 IPO), the *Living Books* series, and [[myst]]. The Computer History Museum's
  profile of Douglas Carlston records him as "founder, CEO and Chairman of Brøderbund
  Software, Inc., an educational software firm, which commenced operation in 1980."
- **Acquired:** by [[the-learning-company|The Learning Company]] on June 22, 1998.
- **Unverified this session:** the sequels' dates, the television show, and whether later
  editions dropped the physical almanac. Not asserted above.
