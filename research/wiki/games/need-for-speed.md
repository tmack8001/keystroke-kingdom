Road & Track Presents: The Need for Speed (EA Canada / Pioneer Productions, December 1994, 3DO)
is older and more serious than the franchise's later reputation suggests: Electronic Arts
partnered with **Road & Track** magazine for precise vehicle data, shipped each of its eight
licensed cars with commentary and real video footage, and built its signature loop around
**police pursuit** on point-to-point roads.

## In this game

Trevor's named "Need for Speed" anchor for **Act 7 — Speed / Racing**
(`research/racing-games-by-era.md` §8). Two findings matter for the spec lane. First, the
*original* is a 1994 quasi-simulation, not the 2000s street racer most people picture — so
"Need for Speed" can occupy either the mid-90s slot or the 2000s slot, but not both meanings at
once. Second, the era-correct, **rating-clean** 2000s pick is
**[[need-for-speed-underground]] (2003)** — which is also the strongest recommended substitute
for the [[grand-theft-auto]] slot the research advises against. *(Ratings correction,
2026-09-13: its **console/PC** ESRB record is **E for Everyone — Mild Language, Suggestive
Themes**. The "E (Mild Lyrics)" descriptor this page previously carried is the **Game Boy
Advance** record. Both are E; the descriptors differ. The rival candidate
[[midnight-club-street-racing]] turned out to be **T on PlayStation 2** and was dropped —
full evidence on that page.)* **This** game's police-pursuit/arrest mechanic must be cut
entirely under the One Rule: authority-figure punishment is the wrong emotional register for a
child — and note that the 2003 game is not built around it, which is part of why it wins the
slot.

## The facts

- Developer: **EA Canada** and **Pioneer Productions**; publisher **Electronic Arts**. Release
  **December 1994** for the Panasonic 3DO; MS-DOS 1995; PlayStation and Saturn 1996; a Special
  Edition in 1996.
  - *Attribution caution:* "Distinctive Software" is often credited. Sources fetched this pass
    credit EA Canada and Pioneer Productions; Distinctive Software was EA Canada's predecessor
    name. Do not use it in a credit line without a stronger source.
- **Road & Track partnership:** the magazine supplied real data about the featured cars, and EA
  matched vehicle behaviour to it — down to reproducing the sounds of each car's gear control
  lever.
- Content: **eight licensed sports cars** (including a Ferrari 512TR and a Porsche 911 (993)),
  each with detailed commentary and real-life video footage; **three point-to-point courses**
  divided into stages, with later ports adding closed circuits and unlockable tracks.
- **Signature mechanic — police pursuit:** evading law enforcement is central. Getting caught
  issues a ticket; **a third ticket results in arrest** (exact timing varied by platform).
- Series direction: the franchise later abandoned the detailed vehicle documentation that
  defined this entry and swung to arcade street racing — **[[need-for-speed-underground]]**
  (2003), rated **ESRB E for Everyone** — *Mild Language, Suggestive Themes* on the
  console/PC record; *Mild Lyrics* on the separate Game Boy Advance record.
