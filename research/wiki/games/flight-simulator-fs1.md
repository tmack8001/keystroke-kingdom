FS1 Flight Simulator — written by **Bruce Artwick** and published by his company **subLOGIC** —
shipped for the **Apple II in January 1980** and for the **TRS-80 later in 1980**. It is the first
entry in the line that becomes [[microsoft-flight-simulator]], and it is the beginning of home
flight simulation: black-and-white **wireframe** graphics, a world of **36 tiles in a 6×6 grid**
(a few hundred square kilometres), and a single aircraft.

## In this game

**The opening station of the proposed flight act** (`research/flight-simulators-by-era.md` §2).
It is the aviation counterpart to what [[monaco-gp]] is for the racing act — the generationally
honest start — and it brings a rendering technique the whole rest of Keystroke Kingdom does not
have: **wireframe line drawing**, strokes with no fills. See [[flight-sim-rendering-eras]].

**Watch the year.** FS1 is frequently dated **1979**, and Artwick did write it in 1979 — but the
Apple II release was **January 1980**. Created-vs-shipped: state 1980 for the release and 1979
only for the writing. *(This is the same error class as Atari's Adventure easter egg — hidden
1979, shipped March 1980; see [[atari-adventure]].)*

**One caution for a children's act:** FS1 was marketed as *"A2-FS1 Flight Simulator with British
Ace — 3D Aerial Battle"* — a WWI dogfight mode was in the box from day one. The flight act should
use the free-flight half and leave the dogfight in the history note. See the combat verdict in
`research/flight-simulators-by-era.md` §6.

## The facts

- **Author:** Bruce Artwick, designer and programmer; publisher **subLOGIC** (also written
  Sublogic), founded in **early 1977** by Artwick and pilot **Stu Moment** after Artwick left
  Hughes Aircraft.
- **The origin is a master's thesis, not a game pitch.** Artwick took a master's in electrical
  engineering at the **University of Illinois** focused on bitmap 3D graphics, completing a thesis
  in **May 1976** on *"a versatile computer-generated flight display"* — developed on a **PLATO
  terminal connected to a PDP-11 minicomputer.** He then wrote up the technique as magazine
  articles in 1976; reader demand for a purchasable program is what produced subLOGIC.
- **Release:** Apple II, **January 1980** (North America); TRS-80 (as **T80-FS1**), later in 1980.
  Both written in the respective machine's assembly language for speed.
- **Rendering:** black-and-white **wireframe**; scenery of **36 tiles in a 6×6 pattern**, roughly a
  few hundred square kilometres, with a single runway, a bridge and a mountain. The Apple II
  version used **composite artifact colours** to widen its palette.
- **Content:** a very basic simulation of one aircraft, **plus the "British Ace" WWI dogfight
  mode** that the packaging advertised. A period reviewer called it *"the single most impressive
  computer game I have seen"*; others called the flying "next to impossible."
- **What it led to:** subLOGIC licensed an IBM PC version to Microsoft in 1982, which shipped as
  **Microsoft Flight Simulator** — see [[microsoft-flight-simulator]].
