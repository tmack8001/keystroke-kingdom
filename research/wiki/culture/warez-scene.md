The "warez scene" — from "wares," short for software — is the underground subculture
of trading commercial software with its copy protection removed ("cracking"), which
coalesced in the 1980s, most visibly around the Commodore 64 in West Germany and the
Netherlands starting around 1983. It organized through dial-up bulletin board systems
and mail-traded floppy disks, and it left behind a genuinely distinctive cultural
artifact: crackers began attaching small animated signature screens — "crack intros"
or "cracktros" — to the software they released, a practice that grew into its own
recognized digital-art form, the demoscene.

## The facts

- **Term meaning:** "warez" (and its leetspeak form "w4r3z") is a plural of "ware"
  (short for software), pronounced like "wares."
- **1980s European origin:** the cracking scene was most active in Europe through the
  1980s and early 1990s, especially around the Commodore 64; the phenomenon is dated
  to roughly 1983 in West Germany and the Netherlands.
- **Distribution:** organized through BBSes ("entire networks of BBSes sprang up to
  traffic illegal software from one user to the next") and by physically mailing
  floppy disks between traders — a route that reportedly let Western software reach
  computer enthusiasts behind the Iron Curtain who otherwise had no access.
- **"Cracking" was called "breaking" in the 1980s** — the act of modifying software
  to remove copy-protection features.
- **Crack intros / cracktros:** cracker groups began attaching animated
  self-promotional screens to the software they released, advertising their group and
  skills — a visual-branding practice that is the direct ancestor of the demoscene, a
  digital-art subculture broadly recognized (including by museums and academic
  writing on games history) as a legitimate creative movement distinct from the
  piracy that spawned it.
