Putting a home console or PC game into a coin-operated cabinet is **an ordinary, documented,
continuously-practised part of the video game business** — not a liberty, not a retro-fantasy.
It runs from Nintendo's own [[nintendo-vs-system|VS. System]] (1984) and PlayChoice-10 (1986),
through Namco and Nintendo's [[mario-kart-arcade-gp|Mario Kart Arcade GP]] line (2005 to the
present), to today's family-entertainment-centre machines built by Raw Thrills / Play Mechanix
and Bandai Namco Amusement out of console and PC franchises. It even runs backwards: Arcade1Up
has sold licensed three-quarter-scale cabinets *into homes* since 2018. **The cabinet is a form
factor, not an era.**

That is **Act 2's existing vocabulary**, verbatim from `.claude/rules/levels/act2.md`:

### The rule, in one line

> **Draw a real thing.** A real cabinet where one exists; the real console, computer or handheld
> where one does not. The only ruled-out option is inventing a coin-op that never existed and
> implying it did.

That is a restatement of the project's standing rule, not a new one — the memory note
*"cabinet art is faithful, not gimmicky"* is about **fidelity to the game**, and was never a
requirement that the game shipped as a coin-op. Both halves of the rule have teeth: fabricating
a "1992 Super Mario Kart coin-op" is the gimmickry it forbids, *and* so is refusing to draw the
SNES that the game really ran on.

## The facts — four documented strands

### 1. Licensed arcade conversions of home titles (1984– )

The founding examples are Nintendo's own, and they are not obscure: the **VS. System** (1984)
put Famicom/NES games into upright and sit-down arcade cabinets — including as **conversion kits
dropped into existing Donkey Kong, Donkey Kong Jr. and Popeye cabinets** — and became the
**highest-grossing arcade machine of 1985** in the United States. **PlayChoice-10** (August 1986)
followed with ten NES games in one menu-driven machine. Full detail and citations:
[[nintendo-vs-system]].

The consequence for Act 7 is concrete: **VS. Excitebike is a real arcade cabinet** (December 1984
Japan / February 1985 North America), so [[excitebike]]'s coin-op form is documented — with the
honest caveat, from Nintendo's own store listing, that it **omits two-player play and the
track-creation mode**, which are two of the most interesting things about the NES game.

### 2. The Mario Kart arcade line (2005– ) — the modern licensed conversion

*Mario Kart Arcade GP* (Namco, published by Nintendo, 2005, Triforce board) was **the first Mario
Kart developed by a third party**, ran **a modified *Mario Kart: Double Dash!!* engine**, and put
Pac-Man characters on a Nintendo grid. Its descendant **Mario Kart Arcade GP DX is a current
commercial product** — Bandai Namco Amusement America's own site lists a seated, rainbow-LED,
42-inch, four-cabinet-linkable machine with wheel and pedals. That is why a modern arcade or a
resort arcade can have a Mario Kart machine on the floor: because Nintendo licensed one and
Namco built it. Full detail: [[mario-kart-arcade-gp]].

### 3. Modern arcade / FEC cabinets of home franchises (2000s– )

The contemporary coin-op industry is largely in the business of turning home IP into big
cabinets. **Play Mechanix** (founded by former Midway designer George Petro) designs; **Raw
Thrills** produces and distributes — a division of labour formalised in **January 2006** when
Play Mechanix approached Raw Thrills for backing on *Big Buck Hunter Pro*. Their licensed output
includes **Minecraft Dungeons Arcade**, described by its own distributor listing as *"an epic,
action-packed adaptation of the popular console and computer game Minecraft Dungeons"* — a
two-player, 42-inch-HD cabinet that **vends a physical collectible card on every play**. (A
collect-them-all loop attached to a cabinet: worth noting beside Keystroke Kingdom's own
Skulltula-style history-card system.) The same partnership's *Halo: Fireteam Raven* is the other
frequently-cited example. **[unverified]** — the Halo attribution rests on a search-result summary
of a company profile this pass could not fetch; state it only as "reported."

Where these machines live is the FEC layer Trevor named: **Dave & Buster's** (a US
restaurant-and-arcade chain whose venues each carry a video arcade, the "Million Dollar Midway,"
and which acquired Main Event Entertainment in 2022), **Round1**, and resort arcades such as
**Great Wolf Lodge**, whose official Northern Lights Arcade pages describe *"the newest and best
games"* for the whole family. **[unverified]**: Great Wolf's own pages **do not name individual
titles**, so "Great Wolf Lodge has a Mario Kart machine" is a first-hand observation (Trevor's,
and a very ordinary one given that the machine is in production) — it is **not** something this
pass could source, and should not be written as a sourced fact.

### 4. The other direction — cabinets built for the home

**Arcade1Up** (founded 2017, first machines 2018) manufactures **officially licensed,
roughly three-quarter-scale** arcade cabinets for home game rooms, carrying the original games'
artwork. The cabinet migrated into the living room at the same time console games were migrating
onto arcade floors. Both movements say the same thing: the cabinet is a **shape people love**,
decoupled from where the software came from.

### The boundary Trevor drew — slots are precedent, not content

Trevor named Las Vegas slot machines as evidence and immediately fenced them off: *"not that we
are building slots or adding gambling into this — just precedent for cabinet builds of classic
at home entertainment games."* Recorded plainly:

- Licensed video-game IP **is** routinely put into casino cabinets: on **26 September 2017**
  Gamblit Gaming announced an agreement with **Bandai Namco Entertainment** for **PAC-MAN Battle
  Casino**, a multiplayer real-money game based on *Pac-Man Battle Royale*, for Gamblit's Model G
  tables. Scientific Games likewise launched a skill-based **Space Invaders** casino game in
  Atlantic City.
- **This pass found no evidence of any Nintendo- or Mario Kart-licensed slot machine.** If that
  is what was remembered, treat it as **[unverified]**; the licensing precedent stands on
  Pac-Man and Space Invaders without it.
- **Out of scope as content.** No gambling mechanics were researched and none should enter a
  children's game. The single usable sentence is: *the cabinet form outlived the arcade era
  badly enough that it turned up in casinos.*

## Corrections from this pass (2026-09-13)

1. **"There is no Super Mario Kart arcade cabinet — do not draw one."** ([[mario-kart]].) The
   narrow claim about the 1992 SNES title survives; the absolute framing does not. See
   [[mario-kart-arcade-gp]].
2. **The deeper process lesson.** The same paragraph that said "do not draw one" *also* said
   *"The faithful object is **an SNES** and a television… or the **cartridge** itself."* The
   right answer was already in the document, buried inside the wrong framing — and the framing
   is what got relayed upward. **A "do not do X" instruction without a "do Y instead" beside it
   invites the reader to conclude there is nothing to draw.** Every prohibition in this wiki
   should ship with its replacement in the same sentence.
3. **"Vegas has Mario Kart slots."** No source found. Licensed video-game slot machines are real
   (Pac-Man, Space Invaders); a **Nintendo-licensed** one is **[unverified]** and, either way,
   out of scope as content.
