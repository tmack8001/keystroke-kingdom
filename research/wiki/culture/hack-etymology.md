The word "hack," in its computing sense, traces to MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club
(TMRC), whose own 1959 club dictionary already defined "hack" (an elaborate,
non-constructive project) and "hacker" (one who makes them) — jargon that transferred
directly into computing when TMRC members began working on the club's early PDP-1.

## The facts

- MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club (TMRC) kept its own dictionary of club jargon; by
  1959, it already defined "hack" as (among several senses) "an article or project
  without constructive end" and "hacker" as "one who hacks, or makes them" — originally
  describing elaborate campus pranks, not computers.
- As TMRC members gained access to MIT's early computers (notably the TX-0 and later
  PDP-1), they carried their railroad-club slang with them — applying "hack" to
  clever, playful, technically impressive programming feats. This transfer is the
  documented bridge between the railroad-club and computing meanings.
- Steven Levy's 1984 book *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution* is the most
  widely cited popular history of this culture and the "hacker ethic," built on
  interviews with TMRC/MIT AI Lab veterans — but Levy documented and popularized the
  culture; he did not originate the term.
