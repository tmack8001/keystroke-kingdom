The dial-up modem "handshake" — the screeching, warbling audio that played over a
phone line while two modems negotiated connection speed and error-correction
settings — was the audible ritual that opened every session of 1990s telnet/BBS/MUD
play, including reaching [[mud|MUDs]] and [[mangband|multiplayer roguelike]] servers.

## In this game

If a Level 20 (or bonus text-adventure wing) scene wants to dramatize "connecting" to
a remote text world, the dial-up handshake sound is the single most recognizable
sensory cue for a parent who lived it — arguably more recognizable than any specific
game referenced. It's already adjacent to existing design: Level 20's narrative beat
is literally about a terminal connecting to something larger (`.claude/rules/levels/act4.md`),
and the Mystery Beat's "PC speaker boot tones" (`.claude/rules/transitions.md`) are
the same idea one era earlier. A modem handshake sound effect (or its rhythm quoted
in a Lyria prompt) would extend that same "the machine is waking up to greet you"
device into the dial-up era specifically. It's also the shared access ritual for the
[[early-graphical-mmorpgs-1990s]] candidates (AOL, CompuServe) as much as for MUDs.

## The facts

- The screeching/warbling sound was produced during the modems' "handshake" — the
  process of two modems testing the phone line and negotiating transmission speed and
  compression/error-correction protocol before data transfer began.
- Hayes (the dominant early consumer-modem maker) pioneered the built-in speaker that
  let users hear this process; the feature was widely copied by competitors, making
  the sound a near-universal shared experience of getting online through the 1980s–90s.
- The sound became obsolete with the shift to broadband in the 2000s, but remains one
  of the most widely recognized audio artifacts of the dial-up internet era.
