Steven Levy's 1984 book *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution* is the
foundational history of hacker culture — chronicling MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club
and early computer-lab hackers through home-computer hobbyists and game designers —
and the book most credited with naming and popularizing "the hacker ethic": the idea
that information should be open and accessible, and that hands-on exploration of how
things work is inherently valuable. It is the same MIT railroad-club culture
documented in [[hack-etymology]].

## The facts

- **Published in 1984** by Doubleday, written by journalist **Steven Levy**.
- **Content:** profiles the people, machines, and events that defined hacker culture
  — from MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club and early mainframe hackers, through
  hands-on hardware builders, to early video-game designers — including early
  profiles of figures like Bill Gates and Steve Wozniak.
- **"The Hacker Ethic":** Levy's book is the most widely cited source articulating
  this idea, distilled to two core principles — (1) all information should be open
  and accessible so people can learn how the world works, and (2) existing knowledge
  should be used to build more knowledge. These principles are presented as emerging
  from the hands-on, exploratory relationship early hackers had with computing
  machines.
- **Later editions:** a 10th-anniversary edition added a new afterword in 1994, and a
  25th-anniversary edition was published by O'Reilly in 2010.
