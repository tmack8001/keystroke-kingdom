The Prussian city of Königsberg (now Kaliningrad) sat on both banks of the River Pregel and
included an island, all stitched together by **seven bridges**. The townspeople's puzzle: can
you take a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once? **Leonhard Euler proved you cannot** —
and the way he proved it is the reason this is one of the most-told stories in mathematics. He
threw away the map. Distances, shapes and street plans are irrelevant; all that matters is
which piece of land connects to which, and **how many bridges meet at each one**. Euler
presented the result to the St Petersburg Academy on **26 August 1735**; the paper, *Solutio
problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis*, was published in **1741**. It is standardly
called **the first paper in graph theory**, and it foreshadows topology.

## The facts

- **The place.** *"The East Prussian city of Königsberg (now part of Russia and known as
  Kaliningrad) straddles both banks of the River Pregel and an island, called Kneiphof,
  located right where the river divides into two branches. In the 18th century, seven bridges
  spanned various segments of the river, connecting different parts of the city."* (Science
  News.) Wikipedia's account has *"two large islands … connected … by seven bridges"* — ⚠️ the
  two accounts differ on how many islands; **four land masses and seven bridges** is the part
  both agree on and the only part a card should state.
- **The date of the proof: 26 August 1735.** Euler *"presented it to members of the St.
  Petersburg Academy on Aug. 26 of that year"* (Science News, of 1735). Wikipedia concurs:
  *"presented to the St. Petersburg Academy on 26 August 1735."*
- **The date of publication: 1741.** The Euler Archive's record for **E53** gives: title
  *Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis*; **Written: 1735**; **Published: 1741**
  in *Commentarii academiae scientiarum Petropolitanae*, Volume 8. Science News agrees:
  *"eventually published in 1741 in the academy's proceedings."*
- **⚠️ Where "1736" comes from.** Neither fetched primary record gives 1736 as the year of
  writing or of publication; Wikipedia's lead sentence uses it (*"In 1736, mathematician
  Leonhard Euler proved that the problem has no solution"*) while its own publication line
  says 1735/1741. The 1736 figure is the *nominal year of the proceedings volume*, a normal
  18th-century backlog artifact — Science News notes the six-year gap *"was not unusual for
  the time."* State 1735, or state nothing.
- **The result.** Euler *"provided the first mathematical proof that such a journey is
  impossible"* (Science News). The Euler Archive: *"Euler reduces the problem to an analysis
  of the degrees of vertices on a graph, eventually determining that the Königsberg bridge
  problem is unsolvable."*
- **The method is the famous part.** The paper's own title — *the geometry of position* —
  shows, per Science News, *"that Euler himself was aware that he was dealing with a new type
  of geometry in which distance and measurement are not relevant."* Wikipedia describes the
  abstraction: land masses replaced by **vertices**, bridges by **edges**, *"abstracted away
  the actual positions and shapes."*
  ⚠️ **Vocabulary caution:** "vertex" and "edge" are the modern restatement. Euler labelled
  the regions with letters; the word **graph** itself is much later (see below). Do not put
  "Euler called them vertices and edges" on a card.
- **Why it matters.** *"Euler's paper arguably marks the beginning of topology and graph
  theory"* (Science News). Wikipedia: his work *"laid the foundations of graph theory and
  foreshadowed the idea of topology."*
