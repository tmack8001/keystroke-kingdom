The Dolch word list (1936, 220 function words) and Fry's word list (a more contemporary
1000-word list drawn from a 5-million-word corpus of 1960s–70s textbooks) are the two
standard early-reader sight-word references in reading instruction. Fry's first 100 words
alone make up roughly half of typical running text.

## In this game

These lists ground decision 7 of Keystroke Kingdom's Act 5 planning: **words are
early-reader decodable** (3–5 letters, phonetic), matching product.md's Word Bank
Principle — "no real words until Level 3... words are chosen to match early reader
vocabulary." Fry's list is explicitly recommended over Dolch by contemporary literacy
researchers (Dolch's list is nearly 90 years old and excludes nouns entirely), which is why
Keystroke Kingdom's own word banks lean toward decodable, phonetic short words rather than
a fixed sight-word list — the design goal is a child recognizing and reading the word they
just typed, which is a slightly different target than Dolch/Fry's "instant recognition
without decoding" goal, but draws on the same underlying research about what early readers
can reliably parse. An open item in `docs/ROADMAP.md` §5 calls for an educator review of
the actual word banks against this literature. This word-level research complements
[[letter-frequency]], the letter-level research behind the key-teaching order those words
are ultimately built from.

## The facts

- **Dolch word list:** published 1936; 220 words; a "service list" of function words
  (the, is, and, etc.) that deliberately excludes nouns.
- **Fry's word list:** more contemporary than Dolch; derived from a corpus of roughly 5
  million words drawn from 1960s–70s textbooks; organized as "1000 Instant Words."
- **Coverage claim:** the first 100 words of the Fry list make up approximately half of
  all running text a young reader encounters.
- Reading Rockets (citing literacy researcher Timothy Shanahan) recommends Fry's list as
  the more contemporary choice over Dolch for current instruction.
