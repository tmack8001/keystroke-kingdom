Between the teleprinter [[oregon-trail|Oregon Trail]] in 1971 and Mattel's dismantling of
[[the-learning-company|The Learning Company]] in 1999, educational software was a real
industry with real houses, real designers, and a real house style. The word "edutainment"
attached to software games in **1983**, in the wake of the video game crash, when developers
pivoted to titles that "would not only satisfy children's entertainment but would please
parents and educators." This page is the synthesis: who made it, what the good ones did
differently, and — honestly — how much of it was drill.

## In this game

Keystroke Kingdom **is** educational software. It belongs to this lineage more than it
belongs to the arcade lineage its cabinets are dressed in. The hub doc
[[educational-software]] carries the placement argument and the card proposals; this page
carries the historical spine those rest on.

The specific inheritance claim is narrow and defensible: the era established **the
simulation-versus-drill split**, and everything Keystroke Kingdom's One Rule says is a
position in that argument. `.claude/rules/product.md` — *"the game should only ever stop a
player's flow with joy, never with punishment"* — is the drill side losing. See
[[error-feedback-pedagogy]] for the motor-learning research that actually grounds the rule
and the framing discipline it requires; this page supplies the historical company, not the
proof.

## The facts

### Where the word came from

- The term **"edutainment" was used in 1983** to describe a package of software games for the
  Oric-1 and Spectrum microcomputers in the UK; the originator was **Chris Harvey** at
  Telford ITEC, a government-sponsored training programme. *(Precision note: this is the
  software-games sense. Broader "edutainment" coinage claims for the 1970s exist and were not
  verified this session — do not assert a single origin without checking which sense is
  meant.)*
- The **1983 video game crash drove the pivot.** The *Boston Phoenix* reported in September
  1983 that "'edutainment' games were a new focus area for companies after end of growth of
  the Atari 2600 software market," and developers moved to "create education games for home
  computer systems which would not only satisfy children's entertainment but would please
  parents and educators."

### The houses

| House | Founded | Note |
|---|---|---|
| [[mecc\|MECC]] | 1973 | State-funded by the Minnesota Legislature; site-licensed to schools; 250,000 copies distributed worldwide by 1982 |
| [[the-learning-company\|The Learning Company]] | May 8, 1980 | Founded by Ann McCormick, Leslie Grimm, Teri Perl and [[warren-robinett\|Warren Robinett]] on a National Science Foundation grant |
| Brøderbund | 1980 | Founded by brothers Doug and Gary Carlston; [[carmen-sandiego]], The Print Shop, Living Books, [[myst]] |
| Davidson & Associates | 1982 | "founded in 1982 by Bob and Jan Davidson, husband and wife, to publish Jan's educational Software" |
| Optimum Resource / Weekly Reader Software | early 1980s | [[stickybear]]; the character was a mascot of Weekly Reader Software, a division of Xerox Education Publications |

Two of the five were publicly seeded (MECC by a state legislature, TLC by the NSF) and one
was founded to publish one teacher's own material. This was not, at the start, a
venture-scale consumer industry.

### The landmarks

- **[[oregon-trail|The Oregon Trail]]** — written 1971, MECC network 1975, Apple II 1980.
  World Video Game Hall of Fame 2016; The Strong calls it "the longest-published, most
  successful educational game of all time," 65 million+ copies.
- **[[rockys-boots]]** — The Learning Company, 1982. Boolean logic as a buildable machine;
  "one of the first educational software products for personal computers to successfully use
  an interactive graphical simulation as a learning environment."
- **[[stickybear-typing]]** — Optimum Resource, 1985. Home keys → full keyboard, learner-set
  difficulty, progress saved to disk, and a mode for typing jokes, riddles and stories.
- **[[reader-rabbit]]** — The Learning Company, 1984, by Leslie Grimm. Early reading, ages 2–8.
- **[[carmen-sandiego|Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego?]]** — Brøderbund, April 23, 1985.
  Shipped with a real almanac; "was not intended as an educational game at release."
- **[[number-munchers]]** — MECC, 1986, by R. Philip Bouchard. Grid, logic rule, Troggles.
- **[[gizmos-and-gadgets|Super Solvers: Gizmos & Gadgets!]]** — The Learning Company, 1993.
  Ages 7–12; build a vehicle from parts won by solving physics puzzles, then race it.
- **Math Blaster!** — Davidson & Associates, **1983**, by educator Jan Davidson with designer
  Richard K. Eckert Jr. "A series of mathematics problems appear on the screen, and the player
  must move to fire the cannon pointing at the correct answer." By November 1985 it had spent
  92 weeks on *Billboard*'s Top Education Computing Software chart, peaking at #2; the series
  became "the best-selling piece of math software in history."
- **[[mavis-beacon|Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing]]** (1987) and **[[mario-teaches-typing]]**
  (1992) belong to the same era and are already documented in [[touch-typing-pedagogy]].

### The honest verdict on drill

**Yes, the era was mostly drill, and the good ones were exceptions.** The evidence, stated
carefully:

- Math Blaster — the best-selling math software in history — is a shooting gallery over an
  arithmetic worksheet. Number Munchers is an evaluate-the-rule drill with pursuers.
  Stickybear Typing's own headline mode, *Keypress*, is explicitly "drills." These are the
  era's commercial centre of gravity.
- **On classroom practice**, Douglas Clements (ERIC ED436231) reports that classroom
  computers were used "mostly for drill-and-practice," while arguing "technology should
  emphasize problem solving" and noting the encouraging trend that "more early childhood
  teachers are selecting more open-ended programs." His framing sentence is the era's whole
  argument in one line: *"Educational technology can change the way children think, what they
  learn, and how they interact with peers and adults, or technology can teach the 'same old
  stuff in the same old way.'"*
- Cynthia Char's 1990 report similarly observes that "advances in software technology have
  resulted in more exploratory, open-ended software programs" for early learners — i.e.
  open-endedness was a *development*, not the baseline.
- The design critique is explicit in the literature on educational games: "Games should take
  the opposite approach of drill-and-practice principles, as this simplifies the games and
  limits the domains of knowledge."

**Scope caution:** Clements and Char describe classroom *use* and early-childhood software
trends. Neither is a census of the commercial catalogue. "The era was mostly drill" is best
stated as: *drill-and-practice was the dominant mode of educational computing, the
exploratory titles were identified as a distinct and newer category, and the titles people
remember with love are disproportionately the exploratory ones.* That is supportable. A
stronger claim is not.

### What the good ones actually did

Drawn from the sourced records of the exceptions above:

1. **The child makes something, and it runs.** Rocky's Boots's circuit kicks or it doesn't;
   Gizmos & Gadgets's vehicle wins or it doesn't. Feedback is the artifact behaving, not a
   score.
2. **Failure returns you to the workshop, not to the start.** Losing a Gizmos & Gadgets race
   sends you back for better parts — the loss is the reason to continue.
3. **The reward is the next thing to do.** Oregon Trail's next river; Carmen Sandiego's next
   country. None of these are point totals.
4. **They sent the child out of the game.** Carmen Sandiego's almanac is the extreme case: a
   printed book required to play.
5. **The learner set the difficulty.** Stickybear Typing: "Choose your own levels of
   difficulty."
6. **Practitioners were in the room.** Reader Rabbit was "developed with input from educators,
   parents, children, and reading specialists"; Math Blaster was written by a teacher; TLC
   started on an NSF grant.

### How it ended

SoftKey took The Learning Company in a **$606M hostile takeover in December 1995** and
adopted its name; it had already bought **MECC for $370M in October 1995**, and bought
**Brøderbund on June 22, 1998 for ~$420M**, cutting 500 Brøderbund staff (42% of the
workforce). **Mattel bought the whole thing in August 1999**, and MECC "shut down in October
1999." Nearly every landmark on this page ended inside one company, and then the company
ended.
