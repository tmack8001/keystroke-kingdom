Two controlled training studies with pre-readers point the same way with an important split:
**handwriting with a pencil beats keyboard typing for learning individual letters**, while
**keyboard typing holds its own at the word level**. The best single data point for a tablet
question is Mayer et al. (2020): 4–6-year-olds trained for seven weeks with a pencil, a tablet
stylus, or a **virtual keyboard on a tablet** — the pencil group learned letters better than the
keyboard group, but the keyboard group matched the pencil group on word reading and word writing
and beat the stylus group. Tapping letters on glass is not a null activity for literacy; it is
just not a substitute for forming letters by hand.

## The facts

- **Mayer, Wallner, Budde-Spengler, Braunert, Arndt & Kiefer (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology*
  10:3054.** Kindergarten children (mean age ≈ 5 years 10 months), **final sample ≈145**, trained
  **16 letters over 7 weeks / 28 sessions (4 days a week)** in one of three conditions: **pencil
  on paper**, **stylus on a tablet touchscreen**, or **typing on a virtual keyboard on a tablet**.
  Results: *letter recognition* — pencil significantly better than keyboard, with better retention
  at follow-up; *letter writing* — no group differences at any time point; *word reading* —
  keyboard better than stylus immediately post-training, pencil not significantly different from
  either; *word writing* — keyboard significantly better than stylus. Conclusion as stated by the
  authors: handwriting with a pencil fosters letter knowledge and visuo-spatial skill relative to
  keyboarding. The stylus group's trouble is attributed to the **low friction of the tablet
  surface** increasing motor-control demands and degrading letter quality.
- **Kiefer et al. (2015), *Advances in Cognitive Psychology*, "Handwriting or Typewriting?"**
  Preschool children trained with pen or keyboard: results "did not indicate a superiority of
  typing training over handwriting training in most tasks"; handwriting was **superior in word
  writing and, as a tendency, word reading**. The authors read this as support for
  action–perception coupling — the motor trace laid down while forming a letter helps later
  recognition. *(Abstract-level: the PMC copy was behind a CAPTCHA for our tooling.)*
- **The nuance that matters for a tablet mode.** In Mayer et al., the tablet condition that did
  *worse* was the one imitating handwriting (stylus). The condition that did *fine* at word level
  was the one that gave up on handwriting entirely and let children **select letters** — which is
  the [[movable-alphabet]] insight arriving from a different direction.
- **Reading on screen vs paper, for the same age band.** Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus (*Review of
  Educational Research* 91(4), 2021) meta-analysed **39 studies in 30 articles, 1,812 children
  aged 1–8**: books that differed *only* by digitisation produced **lower comprehension on
  screen**; digital books with **story-congruent enhancements outperformed paper**; an embedded
  dictionary helped vocabulary but not comprehension; and **an adult reading a print book with
  the child beat a child reading an enhanced digital book alone**. Design quality and the adult
  in the room dominate the medium.
