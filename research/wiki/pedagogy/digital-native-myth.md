"Kids these days are digital natives — they already know how to use this stuff" is a folk belief
the education-research literature rejects. Kirschner & De Bruyckere (*Teaching and Teacher
Education* 67, 2017, 135–142) state it flatly: an information-skilled digital native does not
exist; the competences attributed to that generation "need to be properly taught and acquired,"
the same as any other skill. Designing instruction *as if* the myth were true "hinders rather
than helps learning."

## In this game

This is the guardrail against the most tempting bad argument for a Keystroke Kingdom touch mode:
*"children already type on phones all day, so a tablet mode meets them where they are."* The
evidence says fluency with a device is not fluency with a skill. Two concrete corroborations
live next door: [[touchscreen-text-entry]] (formal ten-finger training gives **no** advantage on
a phone — and, symmetrically, nothing shows phone fluency helps on a keyboard), and
[[keyboarding-readiness]] (children who acquire typing informally use **fewer fingers**, and the
count only creeps up with age). Whatever a glass mode teaches, it will teach because it was
deliberately designed to — not because the child is native to the glass.

## The facts

- **Kirschner & De Bruyckere (2017)**, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 67, 135–142. Two claims:
  (1) "information-savvy digital natives do not exist"; (2) learners do not multitask, they
  task-switch, which harms learning. Educational design assuming either myth hinders learning.
- **The myth's cost is specifically instructional.** The authors' framing is not "screens are
  bad" — it is that assuming innate skill removes the teaching. That maps directly onto typing:
  the Aalto 37k data show mobile typing is "a type of motor skill that people learn on their own
  with no formal training," and the self-taught result is ~36 WPM with thumbs, not expert speed.
- **Nature ran the same argument to a general-science audience** under the headline "The digital
  native is a myth" (Nature 547, 380, 2017). *(We could not fetch it past Nature's auth redirect;
  listed as a search result only.)*
- **What this does NOT say.** It does not say children can't learn on tablets — the app
  meta-analysis in [[childrens-literacy-apps]] finds a real +0.31 SD effect. It says the learning
  comes from the design, not from the demographic.
