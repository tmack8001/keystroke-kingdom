The movable alphabet is a Montessori material — a box of loose letters — that lets a child
**compose words before their hand is ready to write them**. It deliberately separates the
language work (hearing sounds, choosing symbols, spelling) from the motor work (forming
letters). It is roughly a century old, it is used with children aged about 3–6, and it is the
cleanest pedagogical precedent for the interaction pattern that nearly every modern children's
literacy app uses on glass: **pick a letter tile and place it**.

## The facts

- **What it is and what it isolates.** Wooden (or cut-out) letters, used by children roughly
  **ages 3–6**, after they have learned letter shapes and sounds via sandpaper letters. The child
  selects a picture card and assembles a phonetic word — *cap*, *web*, *cup* — by sounding it
  out. The Global Montessori Network describes the isolated skill as "the ability to analyze and
  recognize graphic symbols for phonetic sounds," with the progression running "from letter
  recognition to word formation, to sentences, to stories."
- **Why it precedes the pencil.** The material exists because *the hand develops more slowly
  than the mind*: children can spell long before they have the fine motor control to write. The
  movable alphabet removes handwriting from the task so the child can spend all their attention
  on language and phonics.
- **Primary-source lineage.** Maria Montessori's *The Montessori Method* (1912) describes the
  directress showing children how to compose three-letter words from movable letters, with
  written dictation following afterward — i.e. composition first, writing second. *(We read the
  Internet Archive full text; the phrase "movable alphabet" as such does not appear in that
  translation, which uses "movable letters" / cardboard letters. Treat the primary citation as
  supporting the sequence, not the modern terminology.)*
- **The honest limit.** Composing with tiles is a *reading and spelling* activity. Nothing in the
  Montessori literature claims it produces handwriting or keyboarding skill; it deliberately
  brackets them. Any product borrowing the pattern inherits that limit — and should say so.
