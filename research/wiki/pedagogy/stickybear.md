Stickybear is a children's character created by Richard Hefter who became the mascot of one
of the longest-running educational software lines of the floppy-disk era. The programs were
headed by **Optimum Resource**, and the character served as the mascot of **Weekly Reader
Software, a division of Xerox Education Publications**. Titles shipped from the early 1980s
across Apple II, Atari 8-bit, Commodore 64 and IBM PC, and the line was still being
published for Windows XP/Vista and Mac OS X as late as 2008 — roughly a quarter-century of
the same bear. Uniquely among the era's software houses, Stickybear existed simultaneously
as **picture books and programs**, both from the same author and the same publisher.

## In this game

One of two titles Trevor named as childhood favourites he wants in the game "somewhere /
somehow" (the other is [[gizmos-and-gadgets]]). The line's direct relevance is
[[stickybear-typing]] — a 1985 typing tutor for kids that prefigures four of Keystroke
Kingdom's own design decisions. The wider line matters for two reasons:

- **Era placement.** Stickybear's home is the Apple II, 1983–1988. That is precisely Act 2,
  The Computer Wing (1981–1986), described in `.claude/rules/levels/act2.md` as a room where
  "computers and consoles from different eras line the walls." A beige [[apple-computer|Apple II]]
  with a [[floppy-disk|floppy]] beside it is era-correct set dressing that costs no cabinet
  slot — see the placement discussion in [[educational-software]].
- **The books-and-software pairing.** Keystroke Kingdom teaches reading and typing at once;
  Stickybear is the rare precedent where the same character did both, in print and on disk,
  from the same house. See [[decodable-word-lists]] for the reading side of our word banks.

**Which Stickybear does Trevor actually remember?** Not [[stickybear-typing]], necessarily —
he said so himself. The identification attempt, and its honest verdict (a composite of
Stickybear and [[gizmos-and-gadgets]], with one detail unresolvable), lives on
[[bear-on-a-blue-screen]]. Two titles from this line carry the deciding evidence and have
their own pages: [[stickybear-basketbounce]] (the only one where the player controls the bear)
and [[stickybear-town-builder]] (the only one preserved for MS-DOS — i.e. the only one a dad's
office computer is documented to have run). Anyone drawing Act 2 or Act 3 set dressing from
this memory should read that page first: **no Stickybear title was a side-scroller**, so
drawing one would violate Trevor's own "DRAW A REAL THING" direction.

## The facts

- **Creator:** "Stickybear is a fictional character created by Richard Hefter."
- **Publishing structure:** an edutainment series "headed by Optimum Resource. The character
  was a mascot of Weekly Reader Software, a division of Xerox Education Publications."
  *Naming caution:* Internet Archive object records for early titles credit the imprint as
  **"Weekly Reader Family Software"** (e.g. *Stickybear Basketbounce (1983)(Weekly Reader
  Family Software)(US)*), while Wikipedia says "Weekly Reader Software." Both names appear
  on real artifacts; don't assert one as the only correct form.
- **Timeline:** "Software of the series has been released since the early 1980s," beginning
  on Apple II and expanding to IBM PC, Atari 8-bit and Commodore 64. By 2008 "the most
  recent Stickybear software was developed for Windows XP/Windows Vista and Mac OS X."
- **Dated artifacts** (Internet Archive object records, which carry publisher and year in
  their catalogued titles):
  - *Stickybear ABC* — **1982**, Optimum Resource, Apple II
  - *Stickybear Numbers* — **1982**, Optimum Resource, Apple II
  - *Stickybear Bop* — **1982**, Optimum Resource (Apple II) / Weekly Reader Family Software
    (Atari 8-bit)
  - *Stickybear Basketbounce* — **1983**, Weekly Reader Family Software
  - *Stickybear Math* — **1984**, Optimum Resource
  - *Stickybear Reading Comprehension* — **1985**, Optimum Resource, Apple II
  - *The New Talking Stickybear Opposites* — **1988**, Optimum Resource
  - *Stickybear Math Word Problems, Volume 6* — **1995**, Optimum Resource
- **Breadth of the line:** over thirty programs are catalogued, spanning letter recognition
  (*Alphabet*), arithmetic (*Math*, *Math 2*), literacy (*Reading*, *Spellgrabber*),
  keyboarding ([[stickybear-typing|*Typing*]]), early concepts (*Shapes*, *Numbers*,
  *Opposites*), creative tools (*Drawing*, *Music*), map reading and creative thinking
  (*Town Builder*), preschool omnibuses (*Early Learning Activities*), and even
  programming (*BASIC*).
- **The books were real and concurrent.** Richard Hefter authored Stickybear picture books
  published by Optimum Resource itself — *The Stickybear Book of Weather* (1983), *Lots of
  Little Bears* (1983), *Watch Out!* (1983), *The Stickybear's Scary Night* (1984) — the
  same years as the software. Wikipedia's book list corroborates *Lots of Little Bears*
  (1983) and *Stickybears Scary Night* (1984) and adds *Babysitter Bears* (1983) and
  *Bears at Work* (1983).
- **First-title year: 1982 is now supportable** *(revised 2026-09-13 — this page previously
  declined the claim)*. Three separate 1982-dated Internet Archive artifacts exist —
  *Stickybear ABC*, *Stickybear Numbers* and *Stickybear Bop* — all Apple II, all Optimum
  Resource. Still **not** asserted: which of them was literally first, or a month.
- **Deliberately NOT asserted:** no biographical
  detail about Richard Hefter beyond his authorship credits could be verified — Wikipedia
  "provides no biographical details about Richard Hefter beyond identifying him as the
  character's creator," and the publisher's own site (stickybear.com) returned an empty body.
- **Also unverified:** sales figures, the line's reputation for Apple II hi-res graphics
  (a common nostalgic claim, unsupported by any source found), and which title came first.

## Platforms and disk formats — the spine

*Added 2026-09-13. This is the section that answers "what could a given family machine
actually have run, and in what year." It matters because a childhood memory usually keeps the
machine and the disk more reliably than the title — see [[bear-on-a-blue-screen]].*

- **5.25-inch, 1982 – c.1987.** The whole classic run. Nearly every archived Stickybear
  program states the same requirement: *"runs on any Apple II with 48K"* — a 5.25-inch,
  single-drive machine. The Atari 8-bit releases (Bop 1982; Basketbounce, Shapes 1983; ABC,
  Opposites, Numbers 1984) and the Commodore 64 releases are the same era and the same format.
- **3.5-inch (800K) re-releases, from 1988.** A distinct later wave, for the newer Apple
  machines: *Stickybear Town Builder* 800K, *Stickybear Word Problems* 800K, and the 1988
  re-release of [[stickybear-typing]]. The Town Builder record is explicit — *"This 1988
  re-release is distributed on a single 3.5-inch (800K) disk. It requires an Apple IIgs,
  Apple //c+, Apple //e, or 64K Apple ][+ with a compatible drive controller card."*
  **A 3.5-inch Stickybear therefore means 1988 or later, and an Apple machine.**
- **Apple IIgs talking versions, 1988** — *The New Talking Stickybear Opposites* and *The New
  Talking Stickybear Alphabet*, requiring 512K RAM. The bear learned to speak on the IIgs.
- **MS-DOS: sparsely preserved.** Wikipedia states the line was released for IBM PC (citing a
  1990 *Houston Chronicle* review this pass could not fetch), but the Internet Archive's
  MS-DOS software library holds exactly **one** Stickybear title: [[stickybear-town-builder]]
  (1992). DOS Stickybear is *attested but almost undocumented* — an important caveat for
  anyone reasoning from a memory of an office PC.
- **CD-ROM school editions, 1995–97** — *Stickybear Spelling*, *At Home with Stickybear*,
  *Stickybear Reading Comprehension* (Science and Sports volumes), and *Stickybear Typing:
  A Typing Instructor for Everyone! School Version with Lesson Plans*. Also a Philips **CD-i**
  demonstration disc, and a 1992 Macintosh *Stickybear's Reading Room*.
- **Multi-disk products were the exception.** The only one sourced this pass is *Stickybear
  Printer Picture Library 1* (1986), described in its own manual as *"a two-disk collection
  (red label and blue label) containing hundreds of backgrounds, frames, patterns, pictures,
  and fonts"* — an add-on for the 1985 *Stickybear Printer* poster/greeting-card program. A
  Stickybear that asked you to swap disks was a **creative tool with a clip-art library**, not
  a game.

## What it was actually like

*The texture, assembled from archived packaging, manuals and preservation records. This is
the part that would make an Act 2 shelf object feel true rather than merely accurate.*

- **It came in a box with more paper than disk.** The archived [[stickybear-typing]] retail
  copy contains a disk, a case, a **keyboard reference guide**, a user's manual, **stickers**,
  and a **family poster**. Stickybear Bop and Stickybear ABC were sold with a manual, poster,
  stickers and **punch-out boards**. The software was one object in a small bundle of things a
  child could hold — the stickers and poster were not marketing, they were part of the product.
- **The school copies were plain blue.** Two independent archived retail items — Bop (1982)
  and BASIC (1986) — carry the same collector's note: *"This is in what I assume was a kind of
  bulk-purchase box provided to schools. There is no box art, just a standardized blue box,
  with a sticker on the front."*
- **Dates on one product disagree with each other, routinely.** The Typing package carries
  1986 on the disk label, 1985 on the title screen and 1984 on the case; Math 2's screen and
  manual say 1986 while its case art says 1985; Math Word Problems Vol. 5 is dated from a
  `0795` printing code on the back of the manual. Anyone dating a Stickybear artifact should
  expect a spread, not a year.
- **Most of it was copy-protected; the programming one deliberately was not.** Almost every
  title in the line survives only because crackers (the 4am / san inc preservation project)
  defeated its protection decades later. *Stickybear BASIC*'s manual is the exception and says
  so out loud: *"MAKE A COPY OF THE MASTER DISK! Stickybear BASIC is not copy-protected and we
  suggest that you make a copy of the disk and work with the copy… Stickybear BASIC can be
  copied using COPYA (under DOS 3.3) or the ProDOS System Utilities."*
- **And the manual said the thing this project says.** Stickybear BASIC, 1986, closing its
  introduction: *"The best way to learn a computer language is by using it. Keep experimenting…
  **HAVE FUN! Don't take the computer too seriously.** The more fun you have programming, the
  quicker you will learn. **Don't be disappointed if your program doesn't work on the first
  try, most don't.**"* That is the One Rule (`.claude/rules/product.md`) in 1986 print, from
  the same house that made the typing tutor — and unlike the typing behaviour, this one IS
  sourced. See [[error-feedback-pedagogy]].
- **It was a family game as often as a drill.** Basketbounce's own package copy: *"Keep score
  on the computer to see which player's on top!"* Hot-seat, two players, one keyboard.

## Corrections from citation pass (2026-09-13, BEAR-ID lane)

- **1982, not "early 1980s."** This page previously declined the 1982 first-year claim as
  unsourced. Three 1982-dated archive artifacts were found (ABC, Numbers, Bop). Revised above.
- **"Over thirty programs" understates the preservation record but not the catalogue.** The
  archive holds 240+ Stickybear *items*, but most are duplicate revisions, cracks, boot videos
  and manuals of the same ~30 programs. The "30+ programs" figure stands; do not restate the
  item count as a title count.
- **No Stickybear title in the sourced catalogue is a side-scrolling game.** Recorded here
  because it is a plausible-sounding thing to assume and it is false. See
  [[bear-on-a-blue-screen]].
- **The only preserved MS-DOS Stickybear is Town Builder (1992).** Do not write "Stickybear ran
  on the family PC" as though the DOS catalogue were documented; it is attested by one
  secondary source and one archived program.
