Karaoke's *input* is the human voice and does not translate to a keyboard at all. Its **output** —
lyrics held on screen while a highlight moves through them in syllable-sync — is a different
thing entirely, and it is **the best-evidenced reading-support mechanic anywhere in this
project's research**. The mechanic is 47 years older than karaoke (the Fleischers' **Song
Car-Tunes**, 1924–27, and the "Follow the Bouncing Ball" device that *"bounces across the top of
the words, landing on each syllable when it is to be sung"*), and it has an institutional research
tradition under a different name — **Same Language Subtitling (SLS)**, developed by **Brij
Kothari** at **IIM Ahmedabad** and carried to Indian national television. This page establishes
what SLS actually measured, what it did not, and — the question that decides whether any of it
reaches Oona — **whether the evidence transfers to a five-year-old typing in a browser.** The
short answer: **the mechanic is strongly supported and the population is not ours**, with one
good near-miss study (eye-tracked 4-to-6-year-olds) doing most of the transfer work.

## In this game

**Verdict, carried from [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §9 and sharpened here: adopt the screen, leave the
microphone in 2003.** A Keystroke Kingdom "karaoke cabinet" is a screen where words light up **as
the child types them**. That is not karaoke — it is **assisted reading with a keyboard**, and
naming it honestly is a design requirement, not pedantry: call it karaoke and the first person to
ask *"where's the microphone?"* is the six-year-old.

**Why this outranks the note highway for the low-difficulty default** (see
[[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] §C option 4 for the same conclusion reached from the mapping side):

- **The text stays still and the highlight moves.** Every other rhythm display moves the target
  past the player. For a child whose job is *reading the letter*, those are materially different
  tasks, and the reading-load evidence on clock-driven moving text is thin in both directions
  ([[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] §D). A karaoke line asks the eye to do nothing but track forward
  along stationary print — which is what reading already is.
- **It needs no timing window, no beat map, no receptor line and no calibration.** It is the
  cheapest thing on the entire rhythm-act menu to build, and the only one whose value survives
  `Settings → Sound: OFF` intact (the highlight is visual; the song is optional).
- **It is a reading device that happens to look like a rhythm game**, which is exactly the
  inversion this project wants — not a rhythm game that has been made safe for reading.

### ⚠️ The one substitution that makes it ours — and it is a real departure, so say so

**Every precedent cited on this page is driven by an audio clock.** The bouncing ball lands on the
syllable *the recording is about to sing*. SLS subtitles track *the song*. The e-book narration
tracks *the narrator*. In all of them the machine sets the pace and the child follows.

**This game must invert that: the highlight advances on the child's keystroke, not on a clock.**
That is the sacred wrong-keypress contract (`CLAUDE.md` — *the letter stays on screen and waits*)
applied to a lyric line, and it is what turns a following device into a producing device. It is
also **unprecedented in everything sourced here**, so it should be built as a design decision with
eyes open, not smuggled in under the SLS evidence. What the research supports is *"synchronised
highlighting over held text helps a reader attend to print."* Whether it still helps when the
reader is the one driving the sync is **[unverified] — nobody has studied it.**

## The facts

### 1. The ancestor: the bouncing ball, 1924 — 47 years before karaoke

- Max and Dave Fleischer produced **47 *Song Car-Tunes*** — *"produced and released between 1924
  and 1927"* — the first, ***Come Take a Trip on My Airship***, released **9 March 1924**.
- The series *"pioneered the 'Follow the Bouncing Ball' device used to lead audiences in theater
  sing-alongs."* The device is described as *"a virtual device used in motion picture films… to
  visually indicate the rhythm of a song, helping audiences to sing along"*, in which *"an
  animated ball bounces across the top of the words, landing on each syllable when it is to be
  sung."*
- **That last clause is the load-bearing one**: syllable-level synchronisation of a visual marker
  to held text is a 1924 invention, and it is *precisely* the specification SLS would write down
  seventy-two years later (§2).
- Technique: early entries used a drawn ball *"hitting the words without precise timing"*; the
  mature method filmed **a physical ball on a black baton**, shot on high-contrast film *"that
  rendered the stick invisible."* **16 of the 47** used Lee de Forest's **Phonofilm** sound-on-film
  process from 1925; the other **31 were silent**, for live accompaniment. Fleischer revived the
  form as **Screen Songs (1929–1938)** for Paramount.
- ⚠️ **Small discrepancy, disclosed:** the *Song Car-Tunes* article titles the first short *"Come
  Take a Trip **on** My Airship"*; the *Bouncing ball (music)* article writes *"**in** My
  Airship."* Both fetched the same day. Use "on," note the variant, don't print it as a quotation.

### 2. Same Language Subtitling — what it is, who built it, and when

- **SLS is song lyrics subtitled in the same language as the audio**, on ordinary entertainment
  television, so that a viewer who cannot yet read is repeatedly and involuntarily exposed to text
  they can already *sing*.
- **The specification is explicitly karaoke-shaped:** *"To be really effective, the subtitling
  should have high quality synchronization of audio and text, and better yet, subtitling should
  change color in syllabic synchronization to audio."*
- **Origin:** *"The idea of SLS was first innovated, researched, pioneered and nationalized by the
  Centre for Educational Innovation, Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad under Kothari."*
  **Brij Kothari** conceived it in **1996**; he is an associate/adjunct professor at **IIM
  Ahmedabad since 1996** and president of the nonprofit **PlanetRead**.
- **Three distinct dates, which get compressed and should not be:** **1996** conception → **1999**
  the Gujarati-language pilot on the TV programme *Chitrageet* → **2002** the national step, when
  Doordarshan subtitled *Chitrahaar*; *"the program took off on a national scale by August 2002."*
  From **2006** it ran weekly across **eight** Indian languages; SLS has been implemented on
  Doordarshan film-song programmes in ten listed languages.

### 3. What the evidence actually shows — three tiers, and they are not equal

**(a) Peer-reviewed, on-population, small, and positive.** Kothari, Takeda, Joshi & Pandey,
**"Same Language Subtitling: A Butterfly for Literacy?"**, *International Journal of Lifelong
Education* **21(1), 55–66 (2002)** — ERIC **EJ640415**. Elementary students in India, **138
participants, 46 per condition**: subtitled music video / unsubtitled video / no video, pre- and
post-tested. Finding: *"the best and most sustained improvement was with the use of subtitles"* —
with some gain also in the unsubtitled-video group, which the abstract states plainly.

**(b) The strongest single piece of evidence, and it is NOT the Indian programme.** A **randomized
controlled trial** of SLS in **Kaneohe, Hawaii** was reviewed by the **What Works Clearinghouse**
(ERIC **ED538460**, 2013). The WWC describes the intervention as *"a karaoke-style subtitling
intervention"*. Design: **198 secondary-school students with learning disabilities, aged 14 to
19**, randomly assigned; final analytic sample **51 intervention / 98 comparison**; **12-week**
intervention; reading-comprehension posttests at the end of the intervention (June) and again
after summer break (September). Verdict: the study **"meets WWC evidence standards without
reservations"**, and although the original report did not test significance, *"WWC calculations
indicate that students in the SLS intervention condition scored significantly higher"* on the
reading-comprehension posttests.

> **This matters more than the famous Indian number, and it is the finding to cite.** A
> WWC-without-reservations RCT is the highest evidentiary tier this project has ever attached to
> any mechanic. It is also the tier that makes the transfer problem impossible to wave away —
> §4.

**(c) The famous number, which is the weakest link in the chain.** *"A 2002–2007 Nielsen-ORG study
demonstrated that the ability to read a paragraph among schoolchildren jumped from 25% to 56% when
exposed to 30 minutes a week of the Rangoli program with subtitles."* This reaches us **through
Wikipedia's summary**; the Nielsen-ORG report itself was not located this session, and
**PlanetRead's own site does not mention it** (fetched — it cites an eye-tracking study in
Rajasthan and an AniBook study in Delhi instead). **[unverified] at the numeric level. Do not put
"25% to 56%" on a history card.**

**Institutional recognition, which is real and independent of any single study** (years from the
Kothari article, fetched): **World Bank Development Marketplace 2002** · The Tech Awards Laureate
(Education) 2003 · Ashoka Fellow 2004 · **Schwab Foundation/UNDP Indian Social Entrepreneur of the
Year 2009** · Clinton Global Initiative 2009–2011 · NASSCOM Social Innovation Honour 2011 ·
**USAID All Children Reading Grand Challenge 2012** · **Library of Congress International Literacy
Prize 2013** · iF Social Impact Prize 2017. Google.org is recorded as having selected the work for
funding support.

### 4. ⚠️ Does it transfer to a five-year-old at a keyboard? — four gaps, named

**The honest answer is: the mechanic transfers, the studies don't — and one near-miss study does
most of the work.** Four separate distances between the evidence and Oona:

| Gap | The studies | This game |
|---|---|---|
| **Age** | Indian elementary pupils; US adolescents **14–19** | a child of about five |
| **Prior literacy** | readers who already partly decode, reading *along* | a child learning letters before words |
| **Medium & duration** | broadcast television, 30 min/week, over years | a browser tab, minutes at a time |
| **Agency** | the audio clock moves the highlight; the reader **follows** | the child's keystroke moves it; the child **drives** |

**The one study that closes most of the age gap** — and it is a good one:
**Yow, W. Quin & Priyashri, Sridhar (2019), "Computerized Electronic Features Direct Children's
Attention to Print in Single- and Dual-Language e-Books," *AERA Open* 5(3)** (ERIC **EJ1229675**).
**Seventy-one English-and-Mandarin-speaking 4-to-6-year-olds** read e-books on a computer fitted
with an **eye tracker**. Finding, verbatim: *"enhancing electronic features with synchronized
dual-channel (visual and audio) inputs and attention-guiding cues effectively directed children's
attention to print in both their dominant and nondominant languages."*

- **What that licenses:** for children in exactly this game's age band, a synchronised
  visual-plus-audio tracking cue over text **demonstrably pulls the eye onto the print** — which is
  the whole proximate mechanism a karaoke display is supposed to supply.
- **What it does NOT license:** the outcome measured is **attention to print (gaze), not reading
  gain**. It is also narration-driven, and its participants are bilingual, which is the variable
  the study was actually about. **Do not upgrade "directed attention to print" into "improved
  reading."**

**Three ERIC negatives, recorded as findings rather than omitted** (all queried 2026-09-13):

- `subtitles reading children literacy` → ten results on home literacy environments, parent
  interaction and emergent literacy; **the only caption-adjacent hit is a corpus study of
  children's entertainment vocabulary** (Green, 2023, EJ1383494). **No study of same-language
  captions with emergent readers.**
- `singing software reading fluency` → ten results on text-to-speech, Readers Theatre, drumming
  curricula and reading-assistant software; **none is a karaoke-lyrics reading intervention with
  children.**
- `synchronized highlighted text narration emergent readers` → **seven** total results, of which
  exactly one (the Yow & Priyashri study above) is on point.
- *(Corroborating the hub's own probe: [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §9c recorded that `same language
  subtitling reading` and `karaoke reading fluency` returned nothing useful. That probe was
  under-specified — see Corrections 3.)*

**Net position for the design:** build the karaoke display, and justify it the way
[[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] §4 tells us to justify the whole act — **because it keeps a child reading
and typing voluntarily**, the variable that actually moved in the one study that tested a
rhythm→typing link. Justify it as *reading intervention with a measured effect in five-year-olds*
and you are over-claiming.

## Corrections from citation pass *(2026-09-13)*

1. **The hub's "from 1996" compresses three different dates.** [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §9b reads
   *"Developed by Brij Kothari at IIM Ahmedabad from 1996, piloted on Gujarati TV in 1999, national
   on Doordarshan from 2002"* — which is right, but the underlying articles pin them separately:
   **1996 = conception** (and the year Kothari joined IIMA), **1999 = the Gujarati pilot on
   *Chitrageet***, **2002 = *Chitrahaar* on Doordarshan, "national scale by August 2002."** Name
   the programmes; they are the checkable part.
2. **The syllabic-colour line is a specification, not a description of what shipped.** Wikipedia's
   wording is conditional — subtitling *"should* change color in syllabic synchronization" — so it
   states the ideal, not that every broadcast achieved it. The hub quotes it correctly; anyone
   re-using it must keep the "should."
3. **⚠️ Partly overturns [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §9c's negative finding.** The hub states:
   *"'Karaoke builds reading fluency' is NOT an ERIC-indexed classroom finding."* **ERIC does index
   one** — a **What Works Clearinghouse review of a randomized controlled trial** of *"a
   karaoke-style subtitling intervention"* that **meets WWC standards without reservations** and
   whose effect the WWC itself calculated as significant (ED538460). The hub's probe used
   *"same language subtitling reading"* and *"karaoke reading fluency"* and missed it. **The
   corrected claim is narrower and much more useful: the karaoke-subtitling mechanic HAS a
   WWC-grade classroom finding — for adolescents with learning disabilities, in Hawaii, over 12
   weeks. It still has none for early readers.** Hub file not edited (not this lane's to touch).
4. **Wikipedia's own Nielsen-ORG sentence names the programme.** It is *"the Rangoli program"*, not
   generic "subtitled song programming." Still **[unverified]** at source level, and now with one
   extra reason for caution: **PlanetRead's own current site does not cite it.**
5. **The awards list is verifiable and slightly larger than the hub's.** The hub names World Bank
   2002, USAID 2012, LoC 2013, Schwab/UNDP 2009 and Google.org. All are corroborated, with three
   more (The Tech Awards 2003, Ashoka 2004, NASSCOM 2011, iF 2017) and one softening: Google.org
   is described as having **selected the work for funding support**, which is not the same as the
   hub's flat *"funding from Google.org."*
6. **Title variant on the 1924 short** — *on* vs *in* My Airship, §1.
