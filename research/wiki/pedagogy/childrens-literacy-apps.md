Survey finding, 2026-09-12: **the established children's reading apps do not use a QWERTY soft
keyboard.** Across Endless Alphabet, Duolingo ABC, Teach Your Monster to Read, Khan Academy Kids,
Writing Wizard and Osmo, the recurring interaction vocabulary is **tap-to-select, drag-a-letter-
tile-into-a-slot, trace-along-a-guided-stroke, speak-aloud (ASR), and physical tiles read by the
tablet camera**. That convergence is not an accident: it is the [[movable-alphabet]] pattern,
and it sidesteps every precision problem catalogued in [[keyboarding-readiness]].

## In this game

This is the menu of honest options for a Keystroke Kingdom touch mode — see the hub doc
`research/mobile-touch-typing.md` for how each maps onto our dual read/type promise. The
takeaway for the product lane: **the interesting answer is probably not "a soft keyboard."**
Note also that the efficacy evidence for this whole category is real but modest and easy to
oversell ([[handwriting-vs-typing]] has the letter-level counter-evidence), and that none of
these products claims to teach typing — the one product here that *does* teach typing on glass
(TapTyping, see [[touchscreen-text-entry]]) is an adult tool with no published evaluation.

## The facts — what each product actually makes the child do

- **Endless Alphabet** (Originator Inc., ages 3+) — a word's letters scatter; the child **drags
  each letter back into its shadowed outline**, and each letter animates and says its sound while
  being dragged. A definition animation plays when the word completes. **No keyboard at any point.**
  Common Sense Media frames it as more vocabulary app than alphabet app.
- **Duolingo ABC** (free, ages ~3–8) — "tap, drag, listen, and **speak out loud**": letter
  tracing, bubble-popping, tapping through stories, and **speech recognition** when the child
  reads letter sounds or words aloud. 127 sequential units; no skipping ahead. No QWERTY.
- **Teach Your Monster to Read** (free on desktop; built with Angela Colvert and Alison Kelly,
  senior lecturers in English Education at the University of Roehampton) — the child **matches
  letters to sounds**, then blends and segments CVC words, then reads sentences and little books,
  inside a monster-adventure frame. Stage 1 covers the first 31 letter–sound combinations plus
  CVC words and six sight words, aligned to standard systematic-synthetic-phonics schemes.
- **Khan Academy Kids** (free, ad-free, nonprofit; ages 2–8) — phonemic-awareness play with
  sounds and rhymes, phonics/decoding lessons, read-to-me with word highlighting, and
  **"tracing, letter formation, word building"** for the writing/spelling strand.
- **Writing Wizard** (L'Escapadou) — **guided-stroke letter tracing**: the app shows and enforces
  correct stroke order, with animated rewards on completion, adjustable letter size/difficulty,
  stylus/Apple Pencil support, and printable PDF worksheets so the same letters can be practised
  on paper. *(Mechanics from the developer and store listings; search-result-level.)*
- **Osmo Words** (Tangible Play; ages 5–10) — the tablet sits in a base with a **red reflector
  over the front camera**, which lets the camera see the table in front of the iPad. The child
  spells a hidden word by **laying out physical letter tiles** on the table; correct letters
  appear on screen. This is the movable alphabet with a camera bolted on — screen for feedback,
  hands on real objects.
- **Bookworm** ([[bookworm-game]], PopCap 2003) — the grown-up ancestor: word-building from a
  letter grid, assuming existing literacy. Included here to mark the boundary: tile-building
  games test spelling; they do not teach keys.

## The facts — what the efficacy evidence says about the category

- **Educational apps do work, modestly, and best for exactly this age and skill type.** Kim,
  Gilbert, Yu & Gale (*AERA Open*, 2021) meta-analysed **36 intervention studies / 285 effect
  sizes** for preschool–grade 3 and found a mean weighted effect of **+0.31 SD** on achievement,
  comparable for literacy and math. Effects were **larger for preschoolers than K–3**, larger for
  **constrained skills** (letter knowledge, phonological awareness) than unconstrained ones, and
  **larger on researcher-designed measures than on standardized ones** — that last moderator is
  the reason to be sceptical of vendor-cited effect sizes. The authors stress that "the positive
  overall effect masks substantial variability."
- **Speech input is a weaker tool for young children than it looks.** ASR word error rates on
  children's speech run several times adult rates — one 2023 evaluation reports **13.93% WER on
  children's speech (MyST, grades 3–5) vs 3.05% on adult read speech**, and **21.31%** on scripted
  children's speech, with the gap attributed to developmental variability in pronunciation. A
  speak-aloud mechanic for a 5-year-old will mis-hear them, and in a never-punish game a false
  "wrong" is a design hazard.
