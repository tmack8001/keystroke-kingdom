On September 9, 1947, a moth was found stuck in a relay of the Harvard Mark II
computer and taped into the operations logbook with the caption "First actual case of
bug being found" — the literal origin of the term "computer bug" in the popular
imagination, though not the origin of the word "bug" for technical faults, which
predates this event.

## The facts

- **September 9, 1947, Harvard Mark II:** a technician on the Mark II team finds a moth
  jammed in a relay, removes it, and tapes it into the logbook with the note "First
  actual case of bug being found."
- **Grace Hopper's role is precise and often overstated:** the Smithsonian's own object
  record for the actual logbook states it "was probably not Hopper's." Hopper herself
  repeatedly said in later interviews that she did not personally find or log the moth.
  She was present on the team and spent decades popularizing the anecdote — that
  popularizing role, not authorship of the log entry, is her documented contribution.
  "She didn't find it, didn't write it, and didn't coin the term, but she told the
  story so well for 45 years that it became hers" (Silicon Canals).
- "Bug" as a term for technical faults predates 1947 (Computer History Museum notes
  this); what's novel here is the physical artifact — a literal insect taped into a
  logbook — not the coinage of the word.
- **In-game card text correction:** the Level 13 card's line "She taped it into the
  logbook" is not supported by the sources and should be corrected — see
  [[grace-hopper]] for the full note and suggested replacement text.
