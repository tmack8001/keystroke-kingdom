Dragon's Lair (Advanced Microcomputer Systems / Cinematronics, June 1983) was the first
arcade game to look like a full theatrical cartoon — full-motion laserdisc video animated by
Don Bluth's ex-Disney studio, so visually shocking that players lined up just to watch it
play.

## In this game

Dragon's Lair is the **Level 7 cabinet**, teaching R and U, and the first level of Act 2
(The Computer Wing) after [[donkey-kong]]. It represents a deliberate, dramatic visual leap
— the game's cinematic cel animation is meant to feel as jaw-dropping to the player as it
was in 1983. See `.claude/rules/levels.md` Level 7 and the [[laserdisc-games]] concept page.

## The facts

- Developed by Advanced Microcomputer Systems, published by Cinematronics; conceived by
  Rick Dyer, animated by Don Bluth's studio (ex-Disney animators). Released June 1983.
- Visual style: full hand-drawn cel animation of cinematic, Disney-quality fluidity. Rich
  jewel-toned color grading — deep purples, gold torchlight, emerald dragon scales.
- Gameplay: choose the correct directional/sword input at branching decision points (roughly
  every 1.5 seconds); a wrong or late choice plays a death animation and costs a life.
- Technical: Pioneer laserdisc player for NTSC full-motion-video playback, controlled by
  custom arcade hardware selecting branching video segments by input timing — an
  interactive film, not a raster/sprite/vector game in the traditional sense. ~22 minutes of
  animation, ~50,000 hand-drawn drawings, ~$1.3M of a ~$3M total budget.
- **Correction (50-cent pricing):** famous for charging 50 cents per play instead of the
  standard quarter, reflecting its unprecedented production cost — this is real and
  well-documented, but likely not verifiably the literal *first* game to do so (Missile
  Command briefly shipped with a factory 50-cent default; some accounts also cite Pole
  Position around the same period). Better phrased as "became famous for" rather than "was
  the first" to charge 50 cents.
