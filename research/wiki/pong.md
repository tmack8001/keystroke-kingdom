Pong (Atari, 1972) is the table-tennis simulation that launched the commercial arcade
industry — a pure black-and-white game built entirely from discrete transistor-transistor
logic (TTL) circuits, with no microprocessor, no ROM, and no software of any kind.

## In this game

Pong predates Keystroke Kingdom's Level 1 cabinet ([[breakout]], 1976) but anchors the
[[discrete-logic-era]] concept that defines Act 1's "no anti-aliasing, no gradients, motion
IS the spectacle" visual grammar. It represents the absolute floor of the game's graphic
fidelity ladder — the reference point Level 1's black-and-white rectangles are measured
against.

## The facts

- Developer: Atari — concept assigned by Nolan Bushnell to engineer Allan Alcorn as a
  training exercise; Alcorn designed and built it. Released November 1972.
- Visual style: pure monochrome. Two rectangular paddles, a square ball, a dotted center
  line, numeric score digits. No sprites, no color, no background.
- Input: rotary knob (paddle controller), one per player, analog and continuous.
- Technical: no microprocessor — built from roughly 60–66 TTL chips. No software, no ROM,
  no RAM. All display elements generated directly by discrete logic hardware; the entire
  game existed as a physical circuit. ("No ROM, no RAM" is a sound inference from the
  well-sourced no-microprocessor/no-software fact, not a direct quotation from any source.)
- Paddles could not quite reach the top of the screen — a hardware limitation kept as a
  gameplay feature.
