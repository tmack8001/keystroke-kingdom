**A generic beige 1993 tower cannot carry a sourced machine card — it is a category, not a
product, and nothing this research could fetch says anything true about "a beige PC" in
particular.** What *can* carry one is the thing that turned an anonymous beige box into a
machine a game would run on: the **MPC standard**, a published minimum spec with a
licensed logo you could look for on a shop shelf, set by the **Multimedia PC Marketing
Council** — a working group of the Software Publishers Association whose members included
Microsoft, Creative Labs, Dell, Gateway and Fujitsu. **MPC Level 2 (1993)** asked for a
25 MHz 486SX, 4 MB of RAM, a 160 MB hard disk, a double-speed CD-ROM drive and 44 kHz
16-bit sound. The second sourceable thing about that box is **VGA** (IBM, 2 April 1987),
which every one of them had, and which outlived every other IBM graphics standard.

## The facts

- **Who set it.** Wikipedia's Multimedia PC article: the standard was *"set and named by
  the Multimedia PC Marketing Council (MPMC), which was a working group of the Software
  Publishers Association"*, including Microsoft, Creative Labs, Dell, Gateway and Fujitsu.
  It was an **industry marketing consortium**, not a government or ISO-style standards
  body — worth stating plainly.
- **MPC Level 1 (1991):** 16 MHz 386SX CPU, 2 MB RAM, 30 MB hard disk, 256-colour 640×480
  VGA video, a **1× (single-speed) CD-ROM drive**, and a sound card outputting *"22 kHz,
  8-bit sound"*. Windows 3.0 with Multimedia Extensions was part of the spec.
- **MPC Level 2 (1993):** 25 MHz 486SX CPU, 4 MB RAM, 160 MB hard disk, 16-bit-colour
  640×480 video, a **2× (double-speed) CD-ROM drive**, and *"44 kHz, 16-bit CD quality
  sound."* FOLDOC's independent entry corroborates the same hardware list and adds the
  fine print: the CD-ROM must sustain **300 KB/s** using at most 60% of CPU bandwidth,
  average seek **≤400 ms**, and be **CD-ROM XA-ready**; the audio side needs 16-bit
  digital sound, an 8-note synthesiser and MIDI playback; the display must do 640×480 in
  **65,536 colours**; and MIDI, I/O and joystick ports are required.
- **The badge was the point.** Wikipedia: *"Any PC with the required standards could be
  called an 'MPC' by licensing the use of the logo from the SPA."* The logo on the box is
  the whole mechanism — a parent in a shop could match a sticker on a computer to a
  sticker on a game.
- **It ended quietly.** *"No further specification beyond MPC Level 3 in 1996 was
  published; the Multimedia PC Marketing Council dissolved not too long afterward."*
- **VGA, the other true thing about that box.** Introduced with the **IBM PS/2 line on
  2 April 1987**; **256 KiB of video RAM** on the motherboard; **640×480 in 16 colours**
  and **320×200 in 256 colours**, with palettes drawn from an **18-bit, 262,144-colour
  gamut**. And the reason it is still the answer a beige 1993 box gives: it was *"the last
  IBM graphics standard to which the majority of IBM PC compatible computer manufacturers
  conformed, making it the lowest common denominator that virtually all post-1990 PC
  graphics hardware can be expected to implement."* The Computer History Wiki (gunkies)
  states the same adoption sentence and the same 256 KB / 262,144-colour figures
  independently.
- **⚠️ What is NOT sourced: "the beige box era" as a documented phenomenon.** Nothing
  fetched this session treats beige PC casing as a subject with a history, a reason, or a
  date. It is real, everyone remembers it, and it is **not a citable claim**. Do not write
  a card about why computers were beige.

## Card-ready facts

1. *"In the 1990s a computer could earn a badge that said MPC. It meant the beige box
   really did have a CD drive and a sound card inside, so the game in the shop would
   actually run."* (177 chars) — supports: Wikipedia Multimedia PC **[fetched]** (*"Any PC
   with the required standards could be called an 'MPC' by licensing the use of the
   logo"*) + FOLDOC **[fetched]** (the Level 2 hardware requirements).
2. *"The 1993 rule for calling a computer 'multimedia' was short: a 486 chip, four
   megabytes of memory, a double-speed CD drive, and sound as good as a music CD."*
   (156 chars) — supports: Wikipedia Multimedia PC **[fetched]** (MPC Level 2: 25 MHz
   486SX, 4 MB RAM, 2× CD-ROM, *"44 kHz, 16-bit CD quality sound"*) + FOLDOC **[fetched]**
   (same list, independently).
3. *"Every beige computer of the nineties could show 256 colours at once, picked out of a
   box of 262,144. The standard was called VGA, and it arrived back in 1987."* (153 chars)
   — supports: Wikipedia Video Graphics Array **[fetched]** (2 April 1987, PS/2, 320×200
   in 256 colours from an 18-bit 262,144-colour gamut) + Computer History Wiki
   **[fetched]** (same figures). *Note the precision: 256-at-once is the 320×200 mode;
   640×480 was 16 colours.*
4. *"The rules for a multimedia computer were written by a club of companies, not a
   government — and when the club broke up in the nineties, the rules simply stopped."*
   (159 chars) — supports: Wikipedia Multimedia PC **[fetched]** (MPMC as a Software
   Publishers Association working group; *"No further specification beyond MPC Level 3 in
   1996 was published; the Multimedia PC Marketing Council dissolved not too long
   afterward"*).
