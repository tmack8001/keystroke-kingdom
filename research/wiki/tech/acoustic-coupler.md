The acoustic coupler was a cradle of rubber cups that clamped snugly around an
ordinary telephone handset, letting a computer "talk" to another computer over a
regular phone line as audio tones — no direct electrical connection to the phone
network required. Invented in the early-to-mid 1960s and significantly refined by
SRI International (patented 1966), it was the bridge technology that let modems work
anywhere a telephone existed, including — notably — giving deaf users a way to
communicate by teletype over ordinary phone lines.

## The facts

- **Invented in the early-to-mid 1960s** (one source dates the original device to
  1964); the device clamped a telephone handset into two rubber cups, forming an
  airtight, soundproof seal so tones could pass cleanly in both directions.
- **How it worked:** the modem played encoded tones into the handset's mouthpiece and
  "listened" to the earpiece's response — converting digital data to sound and back,
  entirely through the ordinary telephone microphone and speaker, with no direct
  wiring into the phone network.
- **SRI International's refinements:** SRI scientists significantly improved the
  acoustically-coupled modem (patenting two acoustic couplers in 1966) — including
  circuitry that could reliably pull encoded bits out of the background noise typical
  of long-distance phone lines, and coupler designs that also gave deaf users a way to
  communicate via teletype over standard phone lines.
- **Legacy:** the acoustic coupler let anyone with a telephone connect a computer to
  a remote system — the enabling technology behind dial-up BBSes (see
  [[dialup-modem]]) before direct-connect modems made the rubber-cup cradle obsolete.
