Carl Benz filed for a patent on a "vehicle powered by a gas engine" on **29 January 1886**;
the patent (DRP 37435 — see the numbering caveat below) was granted **2 November 1886** and
is widely called the birth certificate of the automobile. The better story for a child is
what happened two years later: in **August 1888 Bertha Benz** drove the machine from
Mannheim to Pforzheim (~106 km each way) with her two teenage sons and without telling her
husband — fixing it as she went with a hatpin, a garter, a blacksmith, and leather wrapped
around the brake blocks, which is the ancestor of the modern brake lining.

## The facts

- **Patent.** Application filed 29 January 1886; the National Motor Museum (Beaulieu) states
  the patent was "granted on November 2, 1886." **Numbering caveat:** that same museum page
  renders the number as **37345** in one place and **37435** in another; the number cited
  almost everywhere else (including Mercedes-Benz's own corporate history, which would not
  serve an automated fetch in this pass) is **DRP 37435**. Flagged rather than asserted.
- **The machine.** A three-wheeled vehicle with a rear-mounted single-cylinder petrol
  engine, electric ignition, steel-spoked wheels and solid rubber tyres — described by the
  museum as the first car put into series production and the first commercially available
  automobile.
- **Bertha Benz's drive.** On **5 August 1888** Bertha Benz and sons Eugen and Richard left
  Mannheim at dawn for Pforzheim, about **106 km** away (the modern Bertha Benz Memorial
  Route, which includes the return leg via Heidelberg, runs about **194 km**). She is
  credited as the first person to drive an internal-combustion automobile a long distance.
- **The improvised repairs** — all four are corroborated across sources: she bought
  ligroin (a petroleum solvent used as fuel) from a **pharmacy in Wiesloch**, making it
  effectively the world's first filling station; she cleared a blocked fuel line with a
  **hatpin**; she insulated a wire with her **garter**; and a **smith in Bruchsal** repaired
  the drive chain and **covered the brake blocks in leather**.
- **Attribution precision (popularizer vs inventor class).** Many retellings say Bertha Benz
  "invented brake lining." The defensible version: the leather brake covering was done at her
  instigation during the journey and is generally regarded as the first brake lining; the
  work itself was done by the Bruchsal smith. Carl Benz subsequently adopted her suggestions
  — brake pads as standard and an added third gear (needed for the hills she'd had to push
  the car up).
