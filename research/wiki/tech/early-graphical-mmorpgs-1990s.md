A ranked comparison of pre-EverQuest online worlds researched to pin down a specific
childhood memory: **ASCII/text-graphical, overhead like Zelda, genuinely multiplayer
with a persistent shared world, had named clans/alliances/factions you JOINED and
adventured alongside, reminded Trevor of "a really early version of EverQuest,"
reached over dialup (possibly raw telnet), era roughly 1985–1998.** No single
candidate satisfies every cue simultaneously — the research below makes the
trade-offs explicit rather than forcing a false match.

## In this game

**Grounding for a possible Keystroke Kingdom Act 6 "ASCII/overhead text-world"
cabinet, specifically the clan/camaraderie layer.** This request supersedes the
earlier [[nethack]]/[[mangband]] research thread for a *different* childhood memory —
that thread resolved to raw-telnet NetHack, which is explicitly single-player with no
clan system. This memory's defining new detail is **joining a clan/guild/faction and
adventuring together** in a persistent shared world, which no ASCII roguelike or
public NetHack server provides. See each linked entity page for full sourcing.

## The ranked shortlist

### 1. Legends of Kesmai (1996, AOL) — best single-cue match: named guilds you joined
[[legends-of-kesmai]]. Real, sourced, named guilds (SKY, TREE, ROSE) with actual
membership rules — the closest documented match to "clans/alliances you JOINED and
adventured alongside." **Trade-off:** 2D sprite graphics, not ASCII — the visual
upgrade from its parent game [[island-of-kesmai]] is exactly what added the guild
system, so the ASCII cue and the guild cue point at two different (but directly
related) games in the same lineage.

### 2. Island of Kesmai (1985, CompuServe) — best match on ASCII + overhead + persistent multiplayer + EverQuest lineage
[[island-of-kesmai]]. True ASCII/roguelike pseudo-graphics on an overhead grid (the
closest text-mode analog to a Zelda-style top-down map), a genuinely shared
persistent world (not solo-with-spectators), and explicitly credited by Wikipedia as
an ancestor of "EverQuest and World of Warcraft"-style questing design — directly
supporting the "felt like early EverQuest" cue. **Trade-off:** documented social
structure is cooperative *parties*, not a formal named guild/clan system (that's its
sequel, above) — and access was via CompuServe's own menu/terminal software, not raw
telnet.

### 3. Neverwinter Nights (1991, AOL) — best match on scale + "politics, guilds, and alliances" social texture
[[neverwinter-nights-1991]]. GameSpy's own retrospective line — "politics, guilds,
and alliances quickly formed a social community that was far more important than the
actual game" — is close to a verbatim match for the brief's phrasing, and its 2008
Emmy recognition literally groups it with EverQuest and World of Warcraft as an
MMORPG-art-form pioneer. **Trade-off:** mixed text/icon interface (not ASCII), and
access required AOL's own proprietary client software over an AOLnet/SPRINTnet
connection — not raw telnet.

### 4. Meridian 59 (1995–96) — best match on formal factions with real alliance mechanics
[[meridian-59]]. The most mechanically developed faction system found (Necromancer vs.
Hunter, later a three-way political-faction system) — the clearest literal match for
"factions you joined." **Trade-off:** full 3D graphics, a proprietary client, and 1–2
years later than the other candidates — the weakest match on both the ASCII cue and
the earliest-dialup-era timing.

### Ruled out — the MegaZeux/Asciiroth ASCII-RPG-engine realm (2026-07-13 pass)
Trevor separately pointed at three concrete references
(ummagummagames.wordpress.com's ASCII RPG project, [[asciiroth]] at icculus.org, and a
[[megazeux]] community game on DigitalMZX) as "at a minimum... within this specific
realm of feasibility" for the AESTHETIC of an ASCII-overhead RPG world. All three were
fetched and researched directly. **Verdict: this realm is a strong, well-confirmed
match for the visual/interaction aesthetic (overhead or adventure-style movement
through a world built from colored text/font characters) but every documented example
is single-player**, including MegaZeux's own official help documentation stating
network play was "definitely possible... but developers don't have netplay as a high
priority" and never shipped as a stable engine feature (one single-game exception,
*MegaZeux Deathmatch*, 1999, built its own multiplayer inside one game's scripting
layer — not an engine capability). This realm therefore **rules itself out as the
source of the clan/alliance/EverQuest-feeling half of the memory** while strongly
confirming the visual half. See [[megazeux]] and [[asciiroth]] for full detail — this
is exactly the two-hypotheses split described in "The raw-telnet-vs-client
reconciliation" above, now with a third concrete data point: hypothesis (a) — the
clan feeling came from the *community* layer around single-player ASCII games (e.g.
DigitalMZX's forums, shared game archives, a "we all played this together" sharing
culture) rather than in-game networked play — gains real support, since that kind of
enthusiastic hobbyist sharing community is exactly what DigitalMZX and icculus.org
are. Hypothesis (b) — a separate, genuinely multiplayer telnet world is being
conflated with this single-player-but-shared-aesthetic memory — remains equally
possible and unresolved by this pass.

### Other ruled-out candidates
- **Kingdom of Drakkar (1989, CompuServe → MPG-Net 1991):** tile/8-bit graphics with a
  mouse-driven interface (not ASCII); its social structure was open, unnamed
  "join-by-name" parties, not a formal clan/guild system. Fails both the ASCII cue and
  the clan cue. *(search result only, not fetched — Engadget "Game Archaeologist"
  retrospective and MMORPG.com summary)*
- **GemStone III (1990, AOL from 1995):** a pure prose telnet MUD (see [[mud]]) — no
  overhead map or ASCII grid display at all, fails the "overhead like Zelda" cue
  outright regardless of any guild content.
- **Ultima Online (1997):** tile-based isometric graphics from launch, confirmed
  guilds, but explicitly NOT ASCII and NOT telnet-reachable — the request itself
  flagged this as likely-ruled-out on the ASCII cue, and this session's research
  confirms that.
- **NetHack via nethack.alt.org, MAngband:** see [[nethack]] and [[mangband]] — the
  confirmed answer to a *different*, earlier research thread about a raw-telnet,
  spectated-but-not-joined-clans memory. Neither has a clan/guild system a player
  formally joins (NetHack is single-player; MAngband's social layer is PvP looting,
  not named alliances).

## The raw-telnet-vs-client reconciliation

The request explicitly asked to weigh the tension between "raw telnet + ASCII
overhead" (points toward a text/ASCII MUD or roguelike with a map panel) and
"graphical MMO with clans, early-EverQuest feel" (points toward client-based
graphical MMORPGs). **No candidate found this session satisfies both halves at once** —
every game with a documented, named clan/guild/faction system in this era used either
a commercial online service's own front-end (CompuServe menu/terminal software for
Kesmai, a proprietary AOL client for Neverwinter Nights) or a fully proprietary
graphical client (Meridian 59, Ultima Online), never bare `telnet`. This is a real
historical pattern, not a research gap: formal clan/guild *systems* as persistent,
server-tracked social structures were a feature commercial services built and
maintained (worth charging hourly/subscription rates for), while raw-telnet worlds
(MUDs, public roguelike servers) tended toward either no formal membership system
(NetHack) or looser, informal party-naming (Kingdom of Drakkar). **Practical
implication for pinning the exact memory:** if Trevor is confident it was reached by
typing a literal `telnet` command, the clan/guild detail is more likely a slightly
telescoped memory (a later, separate MUD/game experience blending in), or the memory
belongs to a period-appropriate MUD with an informally-run, un-Wikipedia'd
clan/alliance culture that individual MUD codebases and communities absolutely did
build on top of the base engine — LPMud- and DikuMUD-derived MUDs commonly added
custom guild/clan code per-server, but this is per-server modding, not a single
canonical "the game," and could not be pinned to one title this session. If instead
the access memory is softer ("dialed in," "connected," not specifically the word
`telnet`), any of the CompuServe/AOL-era candidates above becomes far more plausible.

## Confirm-cues to pin the exact title

Ask Trevor to check against these specific, distinguishing details:

1. **Access ritual:** Did you type a command like `telnet <address>` at a bare
   terminal/DOS prompt, or did you launch a piece of software (AOL, CompuServe's own
   program, a standalone game launcher) that then connected you? The former points to
   a MUD or public roguelike server; the latter points to Kesmai/Neverwinter
   Nights/Meridian 59.
2. **Visual texture:** Was the screen genuinely monospace text characters (letters
   and symbols standing in for the player, monsters, walls) — or did it have actual
   little pictures/icons/sprites, even simple ones, for characters and the map?
   Monospace text points to Island of Kesmai or a MUD variant; pictures/icons point to
   Legends of Kesmai, Neverwinter Nights, or later.
3. **What you called the clan:** Do you remember any specific guild/clan name, even a
   fragment? "SKY," "TREE," or "ROSE" would confirm Legends of Kesmai directly;
   faction names tied to "Necromancer"/"Hunter" or rival royal houses would confirm
   Meridian 59; anything more free-form ("we just called ourselves the X") is
   consistent with a MUD-community-built guild system this pass could not individually
   pin down.
4. **Service/company name fragment:** Any memory of "AOL," "CompuServe," "Prodigy," a
   green/blue terminal-program name, or a dollar-per-hour cost complaint from a parent
   at the time strongly narrows the service and therefore the game.
5. **Timing relative to LORD:** the brief says "similar to LORD but bigger" — if this
   memory came chronologically close to playing [[legend-of-the-red-dragon]] (a BBS
   door game, see [[bbs-door-games]]), that argues for something also BBS/dialup-native
   rather than a big commercial service — worth checking whether the "bigger" memory
   might in fact be a MUD reached via the same BBS's telnet gateway (see
   [[bbs-door-games]]'s note on the BBS-to-telnet transition), which would favor a
   custom-built MUD guild system over any of the commercial candidates above.

## Design takeaway for Act 6 (2026-07-13)

Regardless of which exact childhood title the clan/EverQuest memory belongs to, the
**MegaZeux/Asciiroth realm is a solid, well-sourced AESTHETIC anchor** for an Act 6
ASCII-overhead cabinet: a DOS/ANSI text-character world the player walks around in,
built from colored glyphs rather than sprite art, with a real, decades-long hobbyist
tradition of building exactly this kind of RPG/adventure ([[megazeux]]'s Caverns of
Zeux lineage and DigitalMZX archive, [[asciiroth]]'s "since 1980" homage, the
ummagumma project). It should NOT be used to justify a multiplayer/clan mechanic in
the cabinet itself — none of these sources support that. If the clan/camaraderie
feeling matters enough to keep, it belongs as a *narrative/social* framing layered on
top of the single-player ASCII aesthetic (e.g. NPC "fellow travelers" the player
meets in the world, echoing DigitalMZX's real sharing-community warmth) rather than
as an actual networked mechanic — fully consistent with the engine having none of its
own game code touched by this research pass.

## Downstream design flags — kid-safety and the One Rule

Every candidate above has real-money commercial mechanics (hourly billing, monthly
subscriptions) and PvP/combat content typical of the genre and era, which have no
place in a kid-facing cabinet regardless of which title is chosen. Follow the
strip-and-reframe precedent already established on [[mangband]],
[[legend-of-the-red-dragon]], and [[nethack]]: **KEEP** the ASCII/overhead
exploration aesthetic (whichever candidate is chosen), the sense of a shared living
world with other people present, and — the genuinely new element this memory
contributes — **the warm camaraderie of joining a named group and exploring together**
(a "clan" reframed as something like a friendly team you're welcomed into, never a
war faction). **STRIP** entirely: PvP/clan warfare, any "reward for killing" mechanic
(explicit in Meridian 59's sourced faction system), permadeath, and any real-money
billing framing. No candidate's guild/clan system, as historically documented, was
designed to be kid-safe as-is — every one of them needs the same "keep the world,
strip the content" treatment already applied elsewhere in this research thread.
