The `=` sign was invented by the Welsh mathematician and teacher **Robert Recorde** in
**1557**, in *The Whetstone of Witte* — the first book published in English to use it,
and (per the National Library of Wales) the book *"in which algebra and the equals sign
(=) are introduced for the first time in published form."* Recorde's own stated reason
survives, in his own spelling: he was tired of writing *"is equalle to"* over and over,
so he chose *"a paire of paralleles, or Gemowe lines of one lengthe"* — **Gemowe meaning
twin** — *"bicauſe noe .2. thynges, can be moare equalle."* Before him, people simply
wrote the word out: *aequales, aequantur, esgale, faciunt, gleich.*

## The facts

- **1557, *The Whetstone of Witte*, Robert Recorde.** National Library of Wales:
  *"'The Whetstone of Witte' was published in 1557 and written by the influential Welsh
  mathematician and teacher Robert Recorde… It is in this book that algebra and the
  equals sign (=) are introduced for the first time in published form."*
- **Recorde's own words, verbatim (original orthography):** *"And to auoide the tediouſe
  repetition of theſe woordes : is equalle to : I will ſette as I doe often in woorke
  vſe, a paire of paralleles, or Gemowe lines of one lengthe, thus: =, bicauſe noe .2.
  thynges, can be moare equalle."* MacTutor (University of St Andrews) quotes the closing
  clause independently: *"bicause noe 2 thynges can be moare equalle."* **Two independent
  sources carry the same sentence, which is why it is safe to quote.**
- **"Gemowe" means twin** (from the same root as *Gemini*). The phrase in Recorde's
  sentence is *"a paire of paralleles, or Gemowe lines of one lengthe"* — a pair of
  parallel, twin lines, the same length. *(The gloss "twin" is this page's reading of the
  quoted word and its Gemini cognate; no fetched source defines it. Flagged.)*
- **It did not catch on immediately.** MacTutor: the symbol *"was not immediately
  adopted,"* with `||` and *ae* (from *aequalis*) still in use **well into the 1700s** —
  roughly 150 years of competition. ⚠️ **Precision matters here: Recorde invented it in
  1557; the world adopted it over the following century and a half.** A card must not
  imply everyone started using it at once.
- **What people wrote before.** Wikipedia's *Equals sign*: before the 16th century
  equality was written out as words — *aequales, aequantur, esgale, faciunt, ghelijck,
  gleich* — sometimes abbreviated to *aeq* or the ligatures ⟨æ⟩ and ⟨œ⟩.
- **The book's other first.** Search summaries state *The Whetstone of Witte* was also the
  first book in English to use the **plus and minus** signs. ⚠️ **Not confirmed by any
  page fetched this session** — and note it would be a claim about *English* usage only,
  not about the signs' invention (they are German and older). Not asserted.
- **Scans exist.** The Internet Archive hosts a scan of *The Whetstone of Witte* (the
  primary work itself), and the Royal Collection Trust holds a copy with an online
  catalogue record. ⚠️ **The RCT record returned HTTP 403 this session** and is not cited
  as read; the Internet Archive item was surfaced in search but not fetched.
