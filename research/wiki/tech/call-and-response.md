Call and response is the older of the rhythm genre's two lineages — **[[touch-me-atari]] (Atari,
1974) → [[simon-electronic-game]] (Milton Bradley, 1978) → [[parappa-the-rapper]] (1996) →
[[space-channel-5]] (1999) → Rhythm Heaven (2006)** — and it is the branch whose **grammar already
contains a wait**: the machine performs, then stops, then the player answers. That makes it the
**better mechanical fit** for a Keystroke Kingdom music cabinet, because it needs nothing removed
from its core loop. **It is not, however, the only workable option, and as of Trevor's 2026-09-13 direction the two
lineages are complementary rather than rival: call-and-response is the gentle default, and the
note-highway-with-degrading-music is what WIZARD turns it into.** See Corrections 0 and 0b below,
and [[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] for the highway route and its evidence. **Two
findings from this pass sharpen the form's own story.** First, the lineage does not uniformly
wait: Touch Me and Simon really do stop and idle, but **PaRappa and Space Channel 5 do not** —
their "wait" is a bar of music, and an answer that arrives late is a miss. Second, **Simon's own
wait is capped**: Milton Bradley's own instruction sheet gives the player **"approximately three
seconds between successive presses,"** so [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §8's *"strip Simon's one-mistake
rule and Simon is already a One-Rule-compliant game"* is **two deletions short**. Corrected below.

## In this game

**This is the part of the rhythm genre the project already promised to build** — which is a
different and better argument than "the only part that survives."
`.claude/rules/product.md`, Level 3 (S & L), reads verbatim:

> - **Music:** Call-and-response melody — left hand (S,D,F) answered by right hand (L,K,J)

and its Content line continues *"full six-key call-and-response rhythm across both hands"*
(`.claude/rules/levels/act1.md` Level 3 carries the same phrase: *"six keys across both hands in
call-and-response rhythm"*). **Confirmed present, quoted exactly, 2026-09-13.** *(Research-only
pass — no file outside `research/` was touched.)*

That is a stronger foundation than it looks, because the left-hand/right-hand framing is not just
a metaphor. Motor-control research measures **inter-keystroke interval (IKI)** directly, and the
established finding is that *"in typing, when the fingers executing two successive movements are
on the same hand, the time between keystrokes is longer than when the fingers are on different
hands"* (Schmuckler & Bosman 1997); Cerni, Longcamp & Job (2016) restate it as **"manual
alternation is performed faster than manual repetition."** Level 3's answering-hands design is
the shape the hands are already fastest in. *(What that does and does not license is worked out
on [[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] — it is a speed finding, not a rhythm-teaching finding.)*

**What is safely adoptable, in descending order of confidence:**

1. **[[touch-me-atari]]'s loop, whole.** Four lit buttons, repeat the sequence, one more each
   round. It is the only station in this lineage with **no response timer documented in its
   cited source** (see Corrections). Delete the three-mistake limit and it is already this
   game's sacred wrong-keypress contract in arcade furniture: the machine shows you a thing and
   waits for you to do it, forever if necessary.
2. **Simon's tone-per-button.** Each button sounds a fixed pitch — the 1978 original an
   **A-major triad in second inversion** — so *playing correctly is itself music*. The project's
   curriculum teaches **50 keys** across Levels 1–25 (counted from `.claude/rules/levels.md`'s
   assignment table), twelve times Simon's four. Assigning pleasant pitches so a correctly-typed
   word makes a phrase interrupts nothing and costs no timing window.
3. **The teacher/student framing of [[parappa-the-rapper]]** — someone performs, you answer —
   **without** its metric deadline and without its COOL/GOOD/BAD/AWFUL ladder.
4. **Rhythm Heaven's mercy, if the series article is right** — a stage becomes skippable after
   three "Try Again" ranks. ⚠️ **Disputed:** the 2006 entry's own article says an "OK" rank is
   *required* to proceed. See Corrections.

**What must not be smuggled in under this name:** a "call-and-response" cabinet that **scores** the
answer's timing. The test is single and blunt — **if the player takes a minute to answer, does
anything bad happen?** If yes, the input contract is broken, whatever the cabinet is called.
*(Note the boundary carefully after Correction 0: a scene may freely **depict** timed
performance — a band playing on, a caller mid-phrase — because depiction is spectacle. The
forbidden thing is the depiction acquiring **consequence**.)*

## The facts

### The musical form (the name is borrowed, and correctly)
- Call and response is *"a compositional technique, often a succession of two distinct phrases
  that works like a conversation in music"* — one musician offers a phrase, a second answers with
  *"a direct commentary or response."* The answer may be *"commentary to a statement, an answer to
  a question, or a repetition of a phrase."*
- Origins are overwhelmingly African: *"in many African cultures, call and response is a pervasive
  pattern"* across civic discussion, religious ritual and music, carried to the Americas through
  the transatlantic slave trade and foundational to gospel, blues, jazz and hip hop.
- **[single-source].** Institutional corroboration was attempted and failed this session:
  `kennedy-center.org`, `si.edu`, `folkways.si.edu`, `loc.gov` and `britannica.com` all returned
  **403**. The definition above rests on Wikipedia alone and should be re-verified before any
  child-facing use.

### [[touch-me-atari]] — Atari, 1974 (arcade); handheld 1978
- *"The player must observe a sequence of blinking electric lights and repeat the sequence back in
  the same order that it occurred."* *"Each time this is completed, the game will produce another
  sequence with an additional button added."*
- Cabinet: *"a short arcade cabinet"* with *"four large circular buttons of the same color."*
- End conditions: *"The game continues until the maximum sequence of buttons is reached, or the
  user makes a mistake"*; the arcade version *"allowed the player to make three mistakes before
  the game ended."*
- ⚠️ **Does it wait? The cited article states no response time limit at all.** That is an absence
  of evidence rather than proof the hardware had none — but it means **Touch Me is the only
  station in this lineage whose documented rules contain no clock.**

### [[simon-electronic-game]] — Milton Bradley, 1978
- Debuted **1978** at **$24.95**, based on *"Atari's unpopular Touch Me arcade game from 1974,"*
  which Ralph Baer's team met at the **Music Operators of America trade show in 1976** and which
  Baer judged *"Nice gameplay. Terrible execution. Visually boring. Miserable, rasping sounds."*
  *(The encounter year conflicts across articles — 1976 here, 1977 on the Touch Me page. Write
  "the mid-1970s"; already disclosed on both existing pages.)*
- Tones: an **A-major triad in second inversion** *"resembling a trumpet fanfare"* — red A, blue
  E, yellow C♯, green E an octave lower; some 1978 units used a **B♭-minor triad**.
- **The wait is capped, and this is the load-bearing fact.** Wikipedia: *"Once the user fails or
  the time limit runs out, the game is over."* And the primary document — **Milton Bradley's own
  Pocket Simon instruction sheet (1980)** — puts a number on it:
  > *"If you fail to repeat a sequence exactly or if you take more than approximately three
  > seconds between successive presses of the lenses during a sequence, POCKET SIMON responds
  > with a 'RAZZ' sound; you have lost and this sequence of signals is over."*
- Two further things the same sheet reveals that the secondary sources omit:
  - **The tempo speeds up on you:** *"After the 5th, 9th and 13th signals in a sequence, POCKET
    SIMON automatically increases the tempo."*
  - **A raspberry is the failure sound.** "RAZZ" is, precisely, the emotional register
    `product.md` forbids ("a gentle nudge sound, not harsh or alarming").
  - Skill levels are sequence-length targets: **8 / 14 / 20 / 31 signals** (levels 1–4); a win is
    *"a rapid series of victory signals from the last lens played."*
- ⚠️ **Scope note:** the three-second figure is from **Pocket Simon (1980)**, a Milton Bradley
  variant, not the 1978 original's own sheet, which was not located this session. The *existence*
  of a time limit on Simon proper is independently stated by the Wikipedia article; only the
  *number* is Pocket-Simon-specific.

### [[parappa-the-rapper]] — NanaOn-Sha / Sony, 1996 — **does not wait**
- *"A bar at the top of the screen will appear, showing symbols that match up to the teacher's
  lyrics"*; the player **times** button presses to answer the rapped line. The answer belongs to
  the next bar of music, not to whenever the player is ready.
- Punishment stack: **Awful / Bad / Good / Cool**; two bad lines in a row demotes; ending on Bad
  or Awful, or dropping below Awful mid-song, **fails the song and restarts it.**
- So its call-and-response *structure* is adoptable and its *clock* is not — the two are
  separable, which is exactly why this title is the most instructive one in the genre here.

### [[space-channel-5]] — Sega, 1999 — **does not wait**
- *"Ulala mimics the movements and vocalisations of her opponents"*, and *"actions are performed
  in time to music tracks playing in each section of a stage."* Pure Simon-says in form, fully
  metric in execution.
- Failure: *"If Ulala makes too many mistakes and loses all hearts during boss battles, fails to
  meet the minimum rating requirements, or causes ratings to drop to zero by missing or failing
  actions, the player reaches a game over."*

### Rhythm Heaven / Rhythm Tengoku — Nintendo SPD, 3 August 2006 (GBA, Japan) — **not actually this lineage**
- *(Plain text, not a wikilink: no `rhythm-heaven` wiki page exists yet — see Issues on
  [[rhythm-games-by-era]]'s follow-ups.)*
- The series *"focuses on audio cues rather than visual cues"*; *"the game primarily relies on
  audio cues to indicate the rhythm."* Its loop is **play in time with a beat**, not **echo a
  phrase back at your own pace.** Its genuinely call-and-response elements are the **Drum
  Lessons** and the pre-stage rehearsals, where the game demonstrates a rhythm before the player
  performs it — a teaching device wrapped around a metric game.
- Rating: **Try Again / OK / Superb**. ⚠️ **Two fetched Wikipedia articles disagree about mercy:**
  the series article says *"the game allows the player to skip levels when players get a 'Try
  Again' rank three times in a row,"* while *Rhythm Tengoku*'s article says *"players must achieve
  an OK rank in order to proceed to the next game."* Plausibly a per-entry difference (the skip
  may have arrived in a later entry); **unresolved, so do not assert either as the series rule.**

### The taxonomy this pass produced
| | Machine performs | Then it… | Late answer costs you? |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[touch-me-atari]] 1974 | blinks a sequence | **waits** (no clock in the source) | **No** |
| [[simon-electronic-game]] 1978 | plays a sequence of lit tones | **waits — but ~3 s per press** | **Yes** (RAZZ, game over) |
| [[parappa-the-rapper]] 1996 | a teacher raps a line | **hands you the next bar** | Yes (grade → demotion → restart) |
| [[space-channel-5]] 1999 | an opponent performs a move | **hands you the next bar** | Yes (ratings → game over) |
| Rhythm Heaven 2006 | demonstrates in rehearsal, then runs a beat | **runs the beat** | Yes (Try Again) |

**Read that table top to bottom and the finding is chronological: the genre spent thirty-two
years putting the clock back in.** The untimed version of this form is the 1974 one.

## Corrections from citation pass *(2026-09-13)*

0. **⚠️ Framing correction from Trevor, relayed mid-lane — this page is no longer a rescue
   argument.** [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §8 originally dismissed a gentled note-highway cabinet as
   *"a different game with Guitar Hero's wallpaper."* Trevor struck it: *"making guitar hero
   'gentle' doesn't defeat that guitar hero inspired the cabinet we made into a typing game —
   after all we aren't byte for byte recreating these original games."* He is right, and the
   project's own standard says so: `.claude/rules/theme-authoring.md` §"Mechanics fidelity"
   requires that **the SCENE show the original game's signature mechanic actually operating** —
   *what the cabinet depicts*, not what the player's hands do. Every shipped level already works
   this way (Breakout's ball is not steered by the player; Tetris's pieces fall while the player
   types; Donkey Kong's barrels are jumped by nobody's thumb). **Consequence for this page:**
   call-and-response remains the better *mechanical* fit — it is the one form that needs nothing
   stripped from its core loop, and it is the one `product.md` already promised — but it is **one
   of two workable routes, not the only survivor.** The note-highway route is worked out on
   [[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]]. *(The hub file was struck by the orchestrator, not by this lane.)*
0b. **⚠️ Second framing update the same day — the two lineages became complementary.**
   `.claude/rules/product.md` §"EXPERIENCE also buys FIDELITY, not just volume" (Trevor,
   2026-09-13) adds that a higher EXPERIENCE tier buys a **more faithful recreation** of the
   original game, with the timing window **degrading the music rather than gating the player**:
   *"have the band/tune fall out of rhythm or out of pitch so it sounds wrong, but you aren't
   punishing the child — they can still advance slower through it."* **Consequence for the two
   lineages: they stop competing.** Call-and-response — the machine performs, then waits — is the
   FIRST QUARTER default, gentle and untimed, and the form `product.md` Level 3 already promised.
   The note highway with a band that drifts out of tune is what the same cabinet becomes at
   WIZARD, opt-in, with nothing taken away. **A single cabinet can be both, and the progression
   between them is itself the difficulty curve.** Evidence that the degradation half has real
   precedent — [[taiko-no-tatsujin]]'s article documenting the genre norm by negating it, and
   [[rocksmith]]'s *"it is impossible for the player to fail a song"* — is on
   [[rhythm-on-a-keyboard]] §0. **The binding constraint still applies here too: fidelity may
   never import a failure state.** Simon's one-mistake rule, its ~3-second timer and its "RAZZ"
   do not come back at WIZARD; what may come back is Simon getting *faster* and the tones getting
   *harder to hold in your head* — more of the original's real difficulty, none of its loss.
1. **⚠️ Load-bearing correction to [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §8.** The hub states: *"Strip Simon's
   one-mistake rule and Simon is already a One-Rule-compliant game."* **Two more deletions are
   required**, both sourced above from Milton Bradley's own instruction sheet: the **~3-second
   inter-press response limit** and the **automatic tempo increase after the 5th, 9th and 13th
   signals**. (A third, softer one: the **"RAZZ"** failure raspberry is the wrong emotional
   register outright.) The hub's §7 ledger row for Simon *does* name the response timer, so the
   ledger is right and only the §8 summary sentence overstates — but §8 is the sentence a
   designer would act on. **Corrected here; the hub file was not edited (not this lane's to
   touch).**
2. **The lineage does not uniformly "wait."** [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §8's framing — *"the machine
   performs; the machine waits; the child answers"* — is true of Touch Me and (with a cap) Simon,
   and **false of PaRappa, Space Channel 5 and Rhythm Heaven**, all three of which require the
   answer inside a musical bar. The correct claim is narrower and better: **the form's 1974–78
   end is untimed; the 1996+ end re-timed it.** That strengthens rather than weakens the hub's §2
   recommendation to open a music act on [[touch-me-atari]].
3. **[[touch-me-atari]]'s "response time limit" is not in its cited source.** Both the hub §7
   ledger row and `touch-me-atari.md` say Touch Me had *"a response time limit"*; the Wikipedia
   article re-fetched 2026-09-13 describes the three-mistake rule and a maximum sequence length
   and **mentions no timer**. Flagged as unsupported-by-the-cited-source, **not** asserted as
   false — arcade hardware of the era plausibly had one, and `arcade-museum.com` (the place to
   check) has been 403 all session. **Whoever next edits `touch-me-atari.md` should soften that
   clause or cite it.**
4. **Rhythm Heaven is a weak fifth link in the chain.** The hub lists it as the lineage's modern
   heir; the fetched articles describe an audio-cue rhythm game whose call-and-response lives in
   its *rehearsals*, not its play loop. Keep it as a cousin, not a descendant.
5. **Rhythm Heaven's skip-after-three-Try-Agains is disputed** between two Wikipedia articles
   fetched the same day (see above). [[rhythm-games-by-era]] §8 point 3 cites it as an escape
   hatch; treat as **[unverified]** at the series level.
