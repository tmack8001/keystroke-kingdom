The QWERTY letter arrangement — the same layout Keystroke Kingdom teaches key by
key — began as a mechanical fix for a jamming problem, patented by Christopher Latham
Sholes, Carlos Glidden, and Samuel W. Soule on June 23, 1868, and set in its modern
form by the time Remington began mass-producing the typewriter in 1873.

## The facts

- Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden, and Samuel W. Soule received U.S. Patent
  No. 79,265 on June 23, 1868 for a "type writing machine" — the first practical,
  mass-producible mechanical typewriter, built in a small Milwaukee, Wisconsin machine
  shop.
- Early typewriters jammed when keys for commonly-paired letters were struck in rapid
  succession, because their type bars swung up from underneath and could collide.
  Sholes rearranged the keys specifically to separate frequently-adjacent letter pairs
  and reduce jamming — not to slow typists down, as a persistent modern myth claims.
- The layout evolved through several iterations between 1868 and the early 1870s (an
  early 1868 version even reversed the alphabet's second half) before settling into
  something close to the modern QWERTY arrangement.
- Manufacturing rights were sold to E. Remington and Sons (better known for sewing
  machines and firearms), who began mass production in 1873 — the point at which the
  layout was locked in and began its spread as a de facto standard.
