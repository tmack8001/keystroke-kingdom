Home PCs could only beep through their built-in speaker — a single square-wave
tone, no music — until add-in sound cards changed that: AdLib in 1987, then Creative
Labs' **Sound Blaster** in 1989, which within a year became the best-selling PC
expansion card and, within a few years, the de facto standard every PC game was
expected to support.

## The facts

- **Before sound cards: the PC speaker.** A stock IBM PC's internal speaker could only
  produce simple square-wave tones — "beeps and boops" — with no capacity for
  multi-voice music or recorded sound.
- **1987 — AdLib.** AdLib released the first widely adopted PC sound card, built
  around the Yamaha YM3812 FM synthesis chip. It offered a 9-voice mode (each voice
  independently programmable) plus a dedicated percussion mode — a real leap from a
  single beeping tone to something resembling an instrument.
- **1989 — Sound Blaster.** Creative Technology (Creative Labs), based in Singapore,
  released the Sound Blaster. It was AdLib-compatible (so existing AdLib-supporting
  games worked on it unmodified) and added a "sound coprocessor" for recording and
  playback of digitized audio, plus a built-in game port for a joystick — a feature
  that mattered because standalone joystick/game-port cards cost around $50 on their
  own at the time, and PCs had few expansion slots to spare.
- **Market dominance, fast.** *"In less than a year, the Sound Blaster became the
  top-selling expansion card for the PC."* By the early-to-mid 1990s, Sound Blaster
  compatibility had become the de facto PC gaming audio standard — a 1994 *Computer
  Gaming World* editorial asked "why would a gamer" buy a non-compatible card, and
  credited *Wing Commander II* (1991) as a major driver of that standardization.
- **Creative's own account corroborates the outline, from the company's side.**
  Creative Technology Ltd's official corporate history page (Singapore, the company's
  home market) states it was "founded in 1981" and, of the Sound Blaster specifically,
  calls it "the invention that gave personal computers the gift of sound — and forever
  changed the landscape of PC multimedia." It dates **Creative Labs Inc.**, the US
  subsidiary that brought the Sound Blaster to the American PC-gaming market, to
  **1988** — the year immediately before the card's 1989 release, consistent with a
  company standing up US operations to launch a US-market product. (This page does not
  give its own release-date figure for the Sound Blaster, so the 1989 date and the
  "top-selling within a year" claim still rest on the Wikipedia references below; the
  company page is cited here for independent corroboration of the founding/timeline
  framing and Creative's own characterization of the product's significance, not as a
  second source for the exact date.)
