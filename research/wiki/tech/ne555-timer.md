The **555** is a tiny analog integrated circuit designed by **Hans Camenzind** as a
consultant to **Signetics**, introduced **1971**, that does one deliberately unglamorous
job: it counts time. Set it up one way and it waits a measured moment; set it up another
and it oscillates — blinking, beeping, ticking. The Computer History Museum calls it
*"one of the most popular and high-volume analog ICs ever made"*; IEEE Spectrum put it in
its **Chip Hall of Fame** and says *"many billions have been sold."* It went into
kitchen appliances, toys and spacecraft, and it is still manufactured today.

## The facts

- **What it is.** IEEE Spectrum describes the NE555 as *"a simple-to-use IC that could
  function as a timer or an oscillator."* That is the whole product: a chip that measures
  time, either once or over and over.
- **Who.** **Hans Camenzind**, working **as a consultant to Signetics** — he was not a
  Signetics staff engineer. Both CHM and IEEE Spectrum name him.
- **When — and the sources disagree by a year, so say so.** CHM's object record gives
  **"Date Introduced: 1971."** IEEE Spectrum says Camenzind *created* the chip as a
  consultant **in 1970** and gives **1971** as the year it hit the market. (Wikipedia,
  not relied on here, says designed 1971 and marketed 1972.) **The defensible card year
  is 1971**, which both fetched institutional sources support as the introduction/market
  year; the *design* year is the disputed one.
- **How many.** IEEE Spectrum: *"many billions have been sold."* It gives **no annual
  figure**, and this page does not either. ⚠️ The widely-quoted *"over a billion produced
  every year"* line appeared only in a **search summary** this session and was **not
  found in either fetched institutional page** — do not put an annual rate on a card.
- **Where it ended up.** IEEE Spectrum: *"kitchen appliances, toys, spacecraft, and a few
  thousand other things,"* plus modern hobbyist electronics and decorative car lighting.
  This is the kid-facing heart of the card: a part small enough to lose in a pocket, in
  the same decade, in a toy and in a spacecraft.
- **It nearly did not exist.** IEEE Spectrum's framing — *"And it almost didn't get
  made"* — is attributed to Camenzind himself, recalling the chip's internal reception a
  few years before his death in 2012. (The article's detail on *why* it almost did not
  get made was not in the fetched excerpt; the bare "it almost didn't get made" line was.)
- **Why it belongs in a computing-history bank at all.** It is an **analog** IC, not a
  processor — CHM files it under *Analog Integrated Circuits*. The bank already cards
  chips ([[intel-386]], [[intel-486]]) and storage ([[flash-memory]]); the 555 is the
  counter-example that a chip need not compute to matter. **⚠️ This page makes no claim
  that the 555 was used in any specific arcade cabinet.** That is plausible and
  frequently asserted; nothing fetched here says it.
