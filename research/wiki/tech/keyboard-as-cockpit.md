Flight simulators are the one major game genre where **the keyboard is not a substitute for a
controller — it IS the instrument panel.** A joystick moves the stick; almost everything else an
aircraft does (flaps, gear, trim, throttle, mixture, brakes, lights, autopilot, radios, radar,
views) is bound to a letter or a number. Publishers shipped printed **quick reference cards** in
the box for exactly this reason. That makes flight sim the strongest in-genre justification
Keystroke Kingdom has ever found for a typing act: in this genre, *typing really is how you fly*.

## In this game

This page exists because Act 7 ("Speed / Racing") is being widened past cars
(`research/vehicle-games-beyond-cars.md`). The racing precedent for typing is a *metaphor*
imported from the browser typing-game canon ([[typing-as-racing]] — TypeRacer's car moves because
you type). The flight-sim precedent is **not a metaphor**: the real 1980s and 90s simulator
already asked the player to memorise several dozen keys and press them at the right moment, and
shipped a card to help. `G` really did lower the gear. An Act 7 flight station can say something
literally true to a child: *"pilots on these games flew with the keyboard, just like you."*

**The real ancestor is not a game.** Apollo astronauts flew to the Moon operating a computer
through a **numeric keyboard**, typing two-number **verb–noun** commands into the
[[apollo-guidance-computer-dsky]]. The line "you operate the machine by typing commands to it"
runs Apollo (1960s) → flight simulators (1980s–90s) → this act. That is not a metaphor the game
is inventing; it is the actual history of how humans flew machines they could not reach inside.

## The facts

**The claim is real, and here is the hard evidence rather than the folklore.**

- **Publishers shipped printed keyboard reference cards in the box.** Two survive as scanned
  primary artifacts on the Internet Archive:
  - **Microsoft Flight Simulator 4.0 (Microsoft, 1989) — "Quick Reference Card."** Catalogued by
    the Internet Archive as "Reference Card for MS Flight Simulator 4.0."
  - **Falcon 3.0 (Spectrum HoloByte / MicroProse, 1991) — "Quick Reference Card."** The scanned
    text of this card lists roughly **80–90 distinct key commands across ~14 categories**: map
    control, stick, throttle/afterburner, weapon selection, radar, view modes (13 separate view
    keys alone), zoom/rotation, countermeasures, flight controls (autopilot, brakes, flaps, gear,
    ejection), AVTR/replay, stores, wingman orders, comms and miscellaneous.
- **Even a light civilian aircraft used dozens of keys.** Jonathan M. Stern's published
  *Microsoft Flight Simulator Handbook* documents **40+ distinct keyboard commands** for ordinary
  flight, grouped as: flight controls (aileron, elevator, rudder, trim), flaps and spoilers,
  throttle and propeller, mixture, EGT bug, instrument calibration, gear and brakes, lights, and
  autopilot.
- **Modern sims are bigger, not smaller.** Microsoft Flight Simulator (2020) ships with several
  hundred default bindings spanning aircraft systems, instruments, camera and drone modes.
  *(A commonly circulated figure is 303 default shortcuts; that exact number is
  **[unverified]** — community shortcut databases, not a Microsoft document.)*
- **It was a genuine barrier, and period accounts say so.** Contemporary retrospectives describe
  1980s sim players having to "memorize what each key did" and note that in most early flight
  sims you could not even take off without reading the manual.

**What this page deliberately does NOT claim.** The romantic version of this story — *plastic
keyboard overlays that sat physically over the keys, shipped in every flight-sim box* — is **not
verified here.** Search results reference a "full colour keyboard map overlay" with Jane's
AH-64D Longbow (1995), but the period review fetched this session does not mention it, and retail
listings are not a source. **Say "printed quick reference cards," which is documented; do not say
"keyboard overlays," which is not.** (This is exactly the plausible-but-invented-justification
class the project keeps catching.)
