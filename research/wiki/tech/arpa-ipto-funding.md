The 1960s–90s computing boom has a funding story, and it is not "public vs private." **ARPA's
Information Processing Techniques Office (IPTO), founded 1962 under J.C.R. Licklider, is estimated
to have funded about 70% of all US computer-science research during his tenure** — money that went
into **universities and private labs** (MIT, Carnegie Mellon, Stanford, SRI), not into a government
computer factory. The honest axis is **motive and time horizon**: long-term, mission-driven
research whose results were then carried into industry by the people it trained.

## In this game

Written to test Trevor's thesis (2026-09-12) that *"in the 60–90s was where all true innovation was
landing and not in the private sector."* **The sources support a sharpened version and refute the
literal one** — see `research/flight-simulators-by-era.md` §11. Two of the era's crown jewels came
out of **private** labs: [[unix]] and C at **Bell Labs**, and the GUI, Ethernet and the modern
mouse at **Xerox PARC** ([[xerox-alto-gui]], [[engelbart-mouse]]). This game's own card bank
already celebrates the Bell Labs one.

What survives, and is a better story anyway: **people built these things to go somewhere and find
something out.** [[arpanet-first-message-1969]] existed to connect researchers, not to sell
anything. [[apollo-guidance-computer-dsky]] existed to land on the Moon. The commerce came after,
built by the students those programmes trained.

## The facts

- **IPTO was established in 1962 within ARPA.** The National Academies' history notes the impetus
  came *"from several directions, including Kennedy's call a year earlier for improvements in
  command-and-control systems."* **J.C.R. Licklider**, a Harvard-trained psychologist, arrived in
  **October 1962**, primarily to run ARPA's Command and Control Group.
- **Licklider's frame was "man-computer symbiosis,"** promoting interactive computing *"with
  missionary zeal to the research community at large."* He concentrated the budget on a handful of
  organisations; **eight of the first nine IPTO-funded organisations in 1963 were working on
  time-sharing.**
- **Scale:** IPTO's budget was **$15 million in 1965**, which *"dwarfed other agencies such as
  ONR."* *"During the 1960s, ARPA and IPTO had more effect on the science and technology of
  computing than any other single government agency."* It is estimated that during Licklider's
  tenure **70 percent of all US computer-science research was funded by ARPA.**
- **Where the money went:** *"the national defense motivation allowed IPTO to concentrate its
  resources on centers of scientific and engineering excellence (such as MIT, Carnegie Mellon
  University, and Stanford University)"* — i.e. **universities, under federal contract.** The same
  pattern produced [[apollo-guidance-computer-dsky]]: NASA contracted **MIT** in 1961.
- **How it reached industry:** the National Academies' account is explicit that young researchers
  trained under ARPA funding *"moved into the workforce"* and that *"the research approaches and
  tools and the generic technologies developed under ARPA's patronage were disseminated more
  rapidly and widely"* as those graduates pursued careers across computing. **The pipeline, not the
  patent, was the transfer mechanism.**
- **The counter-evidence that must stay on the page:** Bell Labs (transistor, [[unix]], C) and
  Xerox PARC ([[xerox-alto-gui]] — the 1973 Alto, the first GUI) were **corporate** research labs
  operating in the same decades. They were, however, *long-horizon* labs of a kind that largely no
  longer exists — which is why the defensible thesis is about **research culture and time
  horizon**, not ownership.
