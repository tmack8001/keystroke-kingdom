ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) was standardized as ASA
X3.4-1963 on June 17, 1963, a 7-bit/128-character code that gave every character a
number — the foundation of both binary text encoding (see [[hello-world]]) and ASCII
art.

## In this game

Level 22's history card spells `\o/` — the backslash unlocks at Level 22, and the
cheering text-person is already in the level's pool. Card text: *"Before pictures,
people drew with letters. ASCII gave every character a number — and gave us little text
people cheering."* ASCII is also the mechanism behind the binary HTML comment that
decodes to `SHALL WE PLAY` and the `01001000...` = HELLO reveal at Level 20 — see
[[hello-world]] and [[hacker-culture]]. See [[computing-history-moments]] for the full
17-card table. ASCII is also the display technology underneath [[roguelike|the
roguelike genre]] (the `@` player glyph, letter-monsters) — see [[nethack]] for the
confirmed reference to a 1990s raw-telnet, publicly-watched ASCII dungeon crawl —
and every plain-text [[mud|MUD]] before [[ansi-escape-codes|ANSI color]] arrived —
the visual language behind a possible Level 20 terminal/text-adventure direction.
[[island-of-kesmai]] (1985) is the strongest researched match combining true ASCII
pseudo-graphics with genuine persistent multiplayer — see
[[early-graphical-mmorpgs-1990s]] for the ranked comparison against later, non-ASCII
graphical MMORPGs with the clan/faction systems a related childhood memory recalled.
[[megazeux]] (1994) and [[asciiroth]] (2009) are a second, single-player-by-design
ASCII-text-character realm — hobbyist game-CREATION systems and their community RPGs,
rather than dungeon-crawl roguelikes or MUD servers — researched 2026-07-13 as a
possible Act 6 aesthetic anchor.

## The facts

- **June 17, 1963:** ASA X3.4-1963 standardized, a 7-bit/128-character code. The
  original 1963 version lacked lowercase letters, added in the 1967 revision.
- ASCII art's lineage runs through typewriter art and teleprinter/TTY transmission into
  the computer era (Kenneth Knowlton, Bell Labs, ~1966) and later BBS culture.
- The `\o/` glyph itself has no documented "origin" as a specific symbol — the card
  doesn't claim it does. What's sourced is the general mechanism (ASCII assigns numeric
  codes to characters) and ASCII art as a well-documented downstream cultural artifact.
- Verdict: accurate for the game's purposes. A minor nuance — the original 1963 version
  lacked lowercase, added in 1967 — is beyond what a children's card needs, noted for
  completeness. No change needed.
