Philips introduced the CD-ROM format in 1984 — adapting the 1981 audio CD to hold
computer data instead of music — and by the early 1990s an affordable CD-ROM drive
let a home PC hold roughly **460 times** as much data on one disc as a standard
1990s floppy: a standard CD-ROM holds 650 MiB against a 3.5-inch floppy's true
formatted capacity of 1440 KiB (the disk's familiar "1.44 MB" label is a mixed-unit
marketing figure, not its real binary capacity — see "The facts" for the corrected
arithmetic).

## The facts

- **1984:** Philips introduces the CD-ROM ("Compact Disc Read-Only Memory") for data
  storage, following the 1981 debut of the music CD. The Computer History Museum's own
  timeline states this plainly: *"For data, the big date is 1984 when Phillips
  introduces the CD-ROM."*
- **Home-PC adoption climbed through the later 1980s into the early 1990s.** The same
  CHM page notes that affordable computer CD-ROM drives went on to enable "multimedia
  encyclopedias, games, novels and reference information on CD." Wikipedia's CD-ROM
  article gives an early-1990s snapshot of that adoption: **"By early 1990, about
  300,000 CD-ROM drives were sold in Japan, while 125,000 CD-ROM discs were being
  produced monthly in the United States."**
- **Capacity: 650 MiB standard, up to 703 MiB.** A standard CD holds 74 minutes of
  audio (333,000 sectors), giving a Mode-1 CD-ROM a net capacity of 650 MiB; 80-minute
  discs (a later, non-Red-Book-standard extension) hold up to 703 MiB.
- **The floppy-disk comparison, computed from two cited figures — corrected this
  citation pass.** The 3.5-inch high-density floppy disk — first shipped 1986, the
  dominant floppy format through the 1990s after IBM's 1987 PS/2 adoption — is the
  correct comparison point for an "early 1990s home PC," not the older 80KB 8-inch
  floppy from 1971 that [[floppy-disk]] already covers. **The "1.44 MB" floppy label
  is itself not 1.44 real megabytes.** Wikipedia's Floppy disk article, re-fetched
  this pass, gives the true figure directly: *"the double-sided, high-density 1.44 MB
  (actually 1440 KiB = 1.41 MiB or 1.47 MB)"* — the "1.44" is a marketing-era mixed-unit
  convention (1024 × 1000 bytes, not 1024 × 1024), not the disk's real binary capacity.
  **Doing the division with the disk's TRUE capacity (1440 KiB = 1,474,560 bytes)
  against the CD's true capacity (650 MiB = 681,574,400 bytes) gives 681,574,400 ÷
  1,474,560 ≈ 462** — not the ~451 this page previously stated, which came from
  naively dividing the two *label* numbers ("650" ÷ "1.44") without noticing one of
  them is a MiB figure and the other is a mixed-unit marketing figure for a smaller
  true value. **"About 460 floppy disks" is the defensible, unit-consistent figure**
  for a card; "~451" is a commonly-repeated but mathematically imprecise shortcut and
  should not be presented as a precise count.

- **The floppy-disk-equivalent arithmetic was wrong and has been corrected.** The
  prior draft computed "650 MiB ÷ 1.44 MB ≈ 451" by dividing the two printed *label*
  numbers without checking that "1.44 MB" is a mixed-unit marketing figure, not the
  floppy's true binary capacity (1440 KiB = 1.41 MiB, per Wikipedia's own parenthetical

  (681,574,400 bytes) ÷ 1440 KiB (1,474,560 bytes) ≈ **462**, not 451. The summary,
  "The facts," and "Card-ready facts" sections were all updated to "about 460" — this
