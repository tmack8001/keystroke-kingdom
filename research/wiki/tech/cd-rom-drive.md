Philips introduced the CD-ROM format in 1984 — adapting the 1981 audio CD to hold
computer data instead of music — and by the early 1990s an affordable CD-ROM drive
let a home PC hold roughly **450 times** as much data on one disc as a standard
1990s floppy: a standard CD-ROM holds 650 MiB against a 3.5-inch floppy's 1.44 MB.

## The facts

- **1984:** Philips introduces the CD-ROM ("Compact Disc Read-Only Memory") for data
  storage, following the 1981 debut of the music CD. The Computer History Museum's own
  timeline states this plainly: *"For data, the big date is 1984 when Phillips
  introduces the CD-ROM."*
- **Home-PC adoption climbed through the later 1980s into the early 1990s.** The same
  CHM page notes that affordable computer CD-ROM drives went on to enable "multimedia
  encyclopedias, games, novels and reference information on CD." Wikipedia's CD-ROM
  article gives an early-1990s snapshot of that adoption: **"By early 1990, about
  300,000 CD-ROM drives were sold in Japan, while 125,000 CD-ROM discs were being
  produced monthly in the United States."**
- **Capacity: 650 MiB standard, up to 703 MiB.** A standard CD holds 74 minutes of
  audio (333,000 sectors), giving a Mode-1 CD-ROM a net capacity of 650 MiB; 80-minute
  discs (a later, non-Red-Book-standard extension) hold up to 703 MiB.
- **The floppy-disk comparison, computed from two cited figures (not a repeated
  "familiar number"):** the 3.5-inch high-density floppy disk — 1.44 MB, first shipped
  1986, the dominant floppy format through the 1990s after IBM's 1987 PS/2 adoption —
  is the correct comparison point for an "early 1990s home PC," not the older 80KB
  8-inch floppy from 1971 that [[floppy-disk]] already covers. **650 MiB ÷ 1.44 MB ≈
  451** — so "about 450 floppy disks" is an honest, sourced-and-computed figure, not
  a vague folk number. (Using MiB vs. MB loosely here, as most consumer-facing
  sources of the era did; the ratio holds regardless to within a few percent.)
