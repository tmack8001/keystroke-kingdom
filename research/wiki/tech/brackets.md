The two keys right of P are `[` and `]` — **square brackets**, always used **in pairs**. In
American English "bracket" alone usually means the square kind; in British English "brackets"
usually means round ones `( )`, which is why this page recommends the unambiguous **"square
brackets"** (two words — but see In this game) in anything a parent might read. In writing,
square brackets mark **words the writer added or changed inside a quotation** (*"I've never been
to this building [the Main Library] this late before."*). In code, **many** languages use them for
**lists** and for picking one item out of a list by number. This page found **no source for who
first drew the mark**; the term "square bracket" is first recorded in English in **1885–90**.

## The facts

### 1. The name — an ambiguity to handle on purpose

- Dictionary.com, **US English**: *"one of two marks [ or ] used in writing or printing to enclose
  parenthetical matter, interpolations, etc."* **British English**: either of a pair of characters
  used to set a passage apart, with a mathematical grouping use; "square bracket" first recorded
  **1885–90.** [Ref 3]
- That "bracket" alone means `( )` in British usage comes from a linguists' mailing-list discussion
  (search result only) — **medium confidence.** [Ref 5]
- Word history: "bracket" is from 16th-century French *braguette* (a codpiece), reaching the
  typographic sense by about 1750 (etymonline search summary; page returned 403 — not read).
  **Do not print** the codpiece etymology on a child card.

### 2. Its job in English

- Writing Commons: *"square brackets—[ ]—are placed around the change"* in a quotation; they are
  *"always used in pairs,"* clarify meaning, add a brief explanation, or fit a quote to your
  sentence; *"A common error writers make is to use parentheses in place of brackets."* [Ref 1]
- University of Edinburgh style guide: *"Use square brackets within a quote to indicate a note
  from the writer that is not part of the original quote."* [Ref 2]
- Early-reader reality: this is advanced punctuation; the card should say **"A note inside a
  quote"** rather than teach editing conventions.

### 3. In code

- Google's Python class: *"List literals are written within square brackets [ ]"* and brackets
  also *"access data, with the first element at index 0."* [Ref 4]
- **Safe wording:** "Many coders use them to make lists." **Not safe:** "every language"
  (C-family languages use `{ }` for some of these jobs; `( )` elsewhere).

## Corrections from citation pass (2026-11-04)

- **Don't call them "brackets" and mean `( )`** in a shared UK/US audience; a parent could read it
  either way. The card shows the glyph, so the risk is low but real.
- Bemer's 1961 ASCII proposal covered `[`, `]` and `\` together (Wikipedia's *Backslash*, see
  [[backslash]]) — this is *standardization*, not the mark's origin.
