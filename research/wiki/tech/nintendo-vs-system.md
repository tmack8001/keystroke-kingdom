The two arcade platforms Nintendo built **out of its own home console** — the **VS. System**
(from 1984) and its successor the **PlayChoice-10** (from August 1986) — are the canonical early
proof that putting a home game into a coin-op cabinet is an ordinary, official industry
practice rather than a fudge. Both run Famicom/NES hardware inside an arcade machine; most of
their libraries are conversions of console games, and a few titles went the other way, debuting
in the arcade before reaching the console. The VS. System became **the highest-grossing arcade
machine of 1985 in the United States**.

## The facts

### Nintendo VS. System (1984–1992)

- **Built on Famicom/NES hardware.** It was **the first version of the Famicom hardware to
  appear in North America**, in 1984 — before the NES console reached the market there — and
  was explicitly intended to preview the console to arcade players.
- **Three cabinet configurations:** upright **VS. UniSystem** (one screen, two sets of
  controls), upright **VS. DualSystem** (two screens, back-to-back), and **sit-down
  DualSystem**. A "Red Tent" sit-down variant supported up to four simultaneous players.
  UniSystems came either as a dedicated grey cabinet **or as a conversion kit dropped into an
  existing Donkey Kong, Donkey Kong Jr. or Popeye cabinet** — a literal, physical example of a
  home library being poured into arcade furniture.
- **The library is console games, altered.** Most titles are Famicom/NES conversions, "some
  heavily altered for the arcade format" (typically made harder); some debuted on VS. before
  the console.
- **Dates and hits:** launch title **VS. Tennis** (January 1984 Japan, March 1984 North
  America); **VS. Excitebike** December 1984 (Japan) / February 1985 (NA); **VS. Super Mario
  Bros.** February 1986, the best-selling VS. release with 20,000 units sold within months. The
  system was **the highest-grossing arcade machine of 1985** in the US.

### PlayChoice-10 (from August 1986)

- **Ten NES games in one cabinet**, playable one at a time, selected from an on-screen menu — a
  showcase machine designed to advertise the NES *and* keep earning arcade revenue.
- **The coin buys time, not a life.** Sessions ran on a time limit with a two-minute minimum;
  inserting more coins added bonus session time ("Prime Time"). Worth noting for this project:
  that is an economic model where **money buys more play rather than punishing failure** —
  closer to Keystroke Kingdom's one rule than the standard coin-op contract.
- **Cabinet variants:** a stacked dual-screen upright (the famous one, with hints on the upper
  screen), a single-screen upright, a smaller "cabaret" version for markets outside the US, and
  a **PlayChoice CounterTop System**.
- **Launch ten:** Baseball, Tennis, Golf, **Excitebike**, Wild Gunman, Mario Bros., **Super
  Mario Bros.**, Duck Hunt, Hogan's Alley, Balloon Fight.
- **Fidelity:** PlayChoice versions used special cartridge-style boards carrying **usually
  unmodified NES games** plus an extra 8 KB ROM of on-screen hints, and output RGB with a
  slightly different palette — so PlayChoice games are generally *more* faithful to the NES
  original than the VS. System's tuned-for-arcade versions.
- Some titles **debuted here before their Western console release**, notably Super Mario Bros. 3.

## Corrections / precision notes (2026-09-13)

- **VS. System production years.** The Wikipedia article's body gives **1984–1992**; an earlier
  search summary this session said 1984–1990. Use **1984–1992**, from the fetched article.
- **"PlayChoice-10 is a multicart."** Close but imprecise: it is ten separate game boards
  selectable from a menu, each carrying a usually-unmodified NES game plus a hints ROM — not one
  combined ROM image.
- **Do not say the VS. versions are "the same game in a cabinet."** They are frequently tuned
  harder, and VS. Excitebike is *missing features the NES version has*. If a cabinet and a
  console appear in the same scene, they are two different objects.
