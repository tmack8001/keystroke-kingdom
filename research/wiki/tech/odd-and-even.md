The oldest surviving definition of **odd** and **even** is also the clearest, and a five-year-
old can act it out with a handful of buttons. Euclid's *Elements*, Book VII, around **300 BC**:
*"An even number is that which is divisible into two equal parts"* (Definition 6), and *"An odd
number is that which is not divisible into two equal parts, or that which differs by an unit
from an even number"* (Definition 7). Share the pile fairly between two people. Nothing left
over: even. One left over: odd. That is the whole idea, in the same words it was written in
twenty-three centuries ago — and it is the first question anybody, human or machine, ever asks
about a number.

## The facts

- **Book VII, Definition 6 (even):** *"An even number is that which is divisible into two
  equal parts."* (Clark University / David E. Joyce's edition of the *Elements*.)
- **Book VII, Definition 7 (odd):** *"An odd number is that which is not divisible into two
  equal parts, or that which differs by an unit from an even number."* Confirmed **twice,
  independently** — Clark University's edition and the Perseus Digital Library's hosting of
  **Sir Thomas Little Heath's** translation, which is the standard English *Elements*. The two
  render it identically, which is why it is safe to quote.
- **Euclid's examples of even numbers**, per Joyce's guide to Definition 6: an even number
  takes the form *b + b*, *"with examples being 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10."*
- **Definition 7 is really two statements, and the second one is a loose end.** Joyce's guide:
  the first part (not divisible into two equal parts) *"serves as the primary definition"*,
  while the second (differing by one unit from an even number) *"is presented as an unproved
  assertion rather than a true definition"* — it needs a proof, and Euclid only uses it from
  Proposition IX.22 onward. A nice, true, and slightly startling fact: **the oldest
  mathematics textbook in the world has a known loose thread in its seventh definition.**
- **The date.** MacTutor (University of St Andrews): *"By the time Euclid's Elements appeared
  in about 300 BC, several important results about primes had been proved."* So **c. 300 BC**
  for the *Elements* — and note the standard caution that this dates the work, not a
  manuscript anyone holds.
- **What is NOT claimed here.** That Euclid *invented* the odd/even distinction. He did not —
  it is far older than him, and earlier Greek arithmetic (the Pythagoreans) already worked
  with it. What Euclid supplies, and what this page cites, is **the oldest surviving written
  definition in the standard text**. Attribute the verb carefully: he *wrote it down*.
