A home dial-up modem plugged into the exact same phone jack and phone line as the
household telephone — and while it was online, nobody in the house could make or
receive a call. This is a distinct, concrete, kid-relatable fact that neither of
this wiki's two existing dial-up pages ([[dialup-modem]], the handshake *sound*, and
[[56k-modem]], the *speed* standard) actually states — it is the reason the modem had
to physically sit near a phone jack, and the reason so many households eventually
paid for a "second line" just for the computer.

## The facts

- **The modem used the household's one phone line — there was no separate data
  line by default.** Wikipedia's Dial-up Internet access article states this
  plainly: *"The simplicity of this arrangement meant that people would be unable to
  use their phone line for verbal communication until the Internet call was
  finished."* Going online and making/receiving a phone call were mutually exclusive
  on a single-line household.
- **This is exactly the limitation broadband was sold against.** The same article
  frames broadband's advantage in direct contrast: *"Broadband does not require the
  exclusive use of a phone line, and thus one can access the Internet and at the same
  time make and receive voice phone calls without having a second phone line."*
- **The industry eventually engineered a partial fix — evidence the problem was
  real and widely felt.** The V.92 modem standard (finalized November 2000, with
  amendments in July 2001 and March 2002) added a feature called **"Modem on Hold"**:
  Wikipedia's V.92 article describes it as addressing the case where an incoming call
  (particularly on a line with call waiting) would otherwise force a disconnection —
  *"This allows the connection to be temporarily severed and then reconnected,
  reducing the possibility of dropped connections."* That a whole feature of a formal
  international standard was built to soften this exact friction is itself evidence of
  how common and disruptive the underlying household problem was.
- **What this page does not (yet) establish:** no source fetched this session gives a
  first-person or journalistic account of the specific "someone picked up the phone
  and knocked me offline" household scene, or names when/how commonly US households
  bought a "second line" specifically for a modem. The three references above
  establish the *mechanism* and the *industry's own acknowledgment* of the problem,
  which is enough to ground a card — but a more anecdotal, human-scale source (a
  Wired/Ars Technica retrospective, for instance) would strengthen it further.
