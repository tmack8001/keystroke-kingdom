ENIAC, unveiled February 14, 1946 at the University of Pennsylvania, is standardly
credited as the first general-purpose electronic computer — a 30-ton machine
programmed by six women mathematicians: Kay McNulty, Jean Bartik, Betty Holberton,
Marlyn Meltzer, Frances Bilas, and Ruth Teitelbaum (née Lichterman).

## The six ENIAC programmers

The correct six, per Penn Engineering's own ENIAC page and the ENIAC Programmers
Project:

- **Kay McNulty** (later Kay Mauchly Antonelli)
- **Jean Jennings Bartik**
- **Frances "Betty" Snyder Holberton**
- **Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer**
- **Frances Bilas Spence**
- **Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum** (maiden name Lichterman, married name Teitelbaum)

**Correction:** an earlier pass of this research listed "Bartik, Holberton, Teitelbaum,
Meltzer, Bilas, Lichterman" as the six — this is wrong, because Teitelbaum and
Lichterman are the *same woman* under maiden/married names. That list therefore named
only five distinct people and silently dropped **Kay McNulty**. The correct six are
listed above. The in-game card text only says "six women mathematicians" without
naming them, so **no change to `js/history-cards.js` was needed** — the naming error
was confined to internal research notes and has been corrected in this document.

## The facts

- **February 14, 1946:** ENIAC unveiled at the University of Pennsylvania, 30 tons.
- Penn's own materials say "first general-purpose electronic computer"; the Computer
  History Museum uses more cautious language — "first large-scale computer to run at
  electronic speed without being slowed by any mechanical parts." The historical
  nuance (Colossus in the UK, the Atanasoff-Berry Computer) hinges specifically on
  "general-purpose" and "programmable," not just "electronic" — ENIAC's claim to fame
  is defensible when qualified as general-purpose.
- The in-game card drops the word "general-purpose," a simplification appropriate for a
  children's game, not a factual error.
- **Weight check:** institutional sources (Penn, Penn Today) consistently cite 30 tons.
  An adult African elephant averages roughly 5.5–6.6 tons; 30 ÷ 5 = 6 tons/elephant,
  squarely inside that range. "Five elephants" is accurate — no change needed.
